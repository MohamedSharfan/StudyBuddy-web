# Unit 1 – Sources of Studying History

> **Textbook pages:** 1 – 9 | **Lessons:** 1.1 Classification of Sources · 1.2 Importance of Learning History · 1.3 Protecting Archaeological Sources

## 1.0 Introduction

- **Sources** are the writings and creations of people who lived in the past. Information about past human activities can be extracted from them, so they are called *sources*.
- When information from the sources is studied logically, the history of people's lives in each period is built.
- Sources are divided into **two** main groups:

| Group | What it includes |
|---|---|
| **Literary sources** | Books and documents written at different times by people of the past |
| **Archaeological sources** | Relics, monuments, coins, inscriptions and ruins unearthed by archaeological excavations and experiments |

---

## 1.1 Classification of Sources

### A. Literary Sources

Literary sources of Sri Lankan history are of two kinds: **local** and **foreign**.

#### (i) Local literary sources
Texts written by Sri Lankans, in prose and poetry, from about two thousand years ago.

**Deepawamsa**
- Considered the **oldest** text of Sri Lanka; written about the **4th century A.D.**
- Contains the history of the country **up to the end of King Mahasen's reign** and the history of the Buddhist order (Sasana).
- Events are recorded **briefly**, and there are **repetitions**.

**Mahawamsa**
- Composed as a chronicle **with fewer mistakes than Deepawamsa**.
- A text with **several parts**. The **first part** was written by **Mahanama Thero**, who lived in the **Diksanda Seneviya Pirivena** (a monastery affiliated to the Anuradhapura Maha Vihara), in about the **5th or 6th century A.D.**
- First part covers the arrival of the Buddha to the end of King Mahasen's reign – similar to Deepawamsa, because both were influenced by earlier texts: **Seehala Attakatha Mahawamsa, Uttara Vihara Attakatha and Vinaya Attakatha**.
- Later writers added information about their own times, so the history of Sri Lanka is written **continuously in one text** – few countries can claim this.
- Regarded as **credible** because many of its events are confirmed by inscriptions and other sources.
- **Mahawamsa Teekawa** (annotation) – written in **Pali** about **four centuries** after the first part; named **Wansattappakasini**; contains information not found elsewhere.

**Other local literary works**

| Type | Examples | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Buddhist religious texts | Bodhiwamsa, Thupawamsa, Dhatuwamsa, Poojawaliya, Saddharmalankaraya | Study of the Rajarata civilization |
| Chronicles | **Nikaya Sangraha** (history of the Buddhist order); **Rajawaliya** (political information) | Gampola to Kotte period |
| Poetry | Sandesha, Prashasthi and Hatan Kawya | Kotte to Kandy period |

**Table 1.1 – Types of ancient literary sources**

| Type of source | Examples |
|---|---|
| Sandesha Kawya (message poems) | Mayura, Thisara, Selalihini Sandesha |
| Prashasthi Kawya (praise poems) | Perakumba Siritha |
| Hatan Kawya (war poems) | Seethawaka Hatana, Constantinu Hatana, Ingirisi Hatana |

![Fig 1.1 – Ancient literary and religious information, including the Wamsakatha, were written on ola leaves](Assets/Chapter_01_Figure_01.png)

**Points to consider when using a literary source (4 questions)**
1. The time in which the source was written
2. The author of the source
3. The author's objectives
4. The way the facts were obtained

> Books from the Anuradhapura era to the start of the Kotte era had mainly **religious objectives**, and some were written **centuries after** the events they describe.

#### (ii) Foreign literary sources
- Sri Lanka traded with foreign countries from the early Anuradhapura era, so traders of the Indian Ocean knew the island.
- Some Indian Tamil books – **Seelappadikaram, Manimekalayi and Padirruppattu** – mention Sri Lanka.
- After the Portuguese arrived (16th century), some foreigners wrote books specifically about Sri Lanka.
- Some writers **visited** the island; others **never came** and wrote from what they heard.

**Uses of literary sources**
1. To prepare the historical chronology.
2. To obtain political, economic and social information of each era.
3. To substantiate information of one source with another.
4. To study the international relations of a country (foreign sources are very important).

### B. Archaeological Sources

Categories: **epigraphy, coins, ruins, drawings, sculptures and antiquities.**

#### (i) Epigraphy
Writings on stone, clay slabs, walls, copper plates, golden plates, wood and urns. Writings on stone slabs or rocks = **inscriptions (Sellipi / Shilalipi)**.

- The oldest inscriptions are in **Brahmi** script, recording events such as **offering caves to Bhikkus**; they help to understand ancient society and the spread of settlements.
- Inscriptions continued from the **2nd century B.C. to the Kandyan kingdom**.
- After the 2nd century A.D. they recorded offering premises to temples, temple administration, government laws, taxes, trading, pardon of convicts and appreciation of service.

|---|---|
| Stone | King Nissankamalla's **Galpotha** inscription (Polonnaruwa) |
| Walls | The graffiti in **Sigiriya** |
| Copper plate | **Panakaduwa Copper Plate** of King Vijayabahu I |
| Golden plate | **Vallipuram** golden plate |
| Wood | Writings on wooden posts of Ambekke Devalaya |
| Clay | Scripts on tiles and bricks |
| Urns | Scripts on clay pots and begging bowls |

#### (ii) Coins
- Used from the Anuradhapura era. The oldest are **Kahapana** (also *Purana, Dharana*).
- Types: **tusker and Swastika** coins, **Lakshmi** coins, a gold coin called **Aka**; **copper coins (Thamba Massa)** in the Polonnaruwa kingdom.
- Coins were made locally (even **moulds** have been found); **Roman, Chinese and Indian** coins are also found – evidence of trade.
- Study of ancient coins = **numismatics**. Coins reveal the economy, trade and use of metals.

![Fig 1.5 – A medieval gold coin (Madyakalina Ran Kahawanu); a source for the economy and technology](Assets/Chapter_01_Figure_05.png)

#### (iii) Ancient ruins
Buildings, stupas, stone pillars, tanks and ponds. They show **creative ability, technology, architecture, craftsmanship, environmental conservation and water management**.

#### (iv) Drawings, sculpture and antiquities
- Show clothes, art skills, jewellery, colours and religious beliefs – e.g. the **Sigiriya frescoes** show women's clothes, jewellery, hair styles and beauty culture.
- **Antiquities** = objects made or used by ancient people; kept in museums, religious places and by individuals; many found in excavations.

![Fig 1.6 – The 'Man and Horse's Head' sculpture at Isurumuni, Anuradhapura (7th–8th century A.D.): Agni (horse's head) and Parjanya (man)](Assets/Chapter_01_Figure_06.png)

### Literary vs archaeological sources

| Feature | Literary sources | Archaeological sources |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Reports of past events | Physical remains |
| Time of creation | Often **later** than the events | Mostly **contemporary** with the events |
| Bias | Authors' personal likes and dislikes may enter | Less affected by bias |
| Example | Mahawamsa, Deepawamsa | Inscriptions, coins, Sigiriya frescoes |

> If a literary account is **substantiated** by archaeological evidence, it is considered **credible**. Use **all** sources, analyse them critically, and build history from the most credible information.

## 1.2 Importance of Learning History
Reading history develops a **critical sense**, logical thinking and creative problem solving, and builds personality. Benefits:
1. Proper understanding of the society and world one lives in.
2. Building the future by understanding the present through past experience.
3. Recognizing the **national identity**.
4. Building **national reconciliation** by loving humanity.
5. Respecting others' cultures.
6. Tolerating different views.

---

## 1.3 Protecting Archaeological Sources
- Only works of **long-lasting materials** (stone, metal, clay) and large works (tanks, canals) survive.
- **Threats:** speedy urbanization, expansion of settlements, road and building construction, agriculture, **treasure hunting**, and scribbling or drawing on monuments.
- Marks carved on rocks (Sun, Moon, Palmyra-fan, bow, arrow, conch, ox-hoof, tusker-step, animals) are **ancient border marks** or an ancient form of **communication like road signs**; moonstones and guard stones are also antiquities.
- Destroying them is a **punishable offence**; protecting them is the duty of every citizen.

### ⭐ Important Facts
1. Things that help learn history are **sources**: literary and archaeological.
2. Books and documents = literary; ruins and antiques = archaeological.
3. Learning history shows how ancestors faced experiences, giving knowledge to face similar problems.
4. A country's history reflects the **identity** of its citizens.

# Unit 2 – Ancient Settlements

> **Textbook pages:** 10 – 30 | **Lessons:** 2.1 Settlements in the Pre-historic Era · 2.2 Settlements in the Proto-historic Era · 2.3 Settlements in the Early Historic Era

## 2.0 Introduction
- Sri Lanka became a human habitation about **125 000 years ago**. **Homo Sapiens** (modern man) migrated across the Indian Ocean and adapted to different environmental zones.
- Because of cultural change, human history in Sri Lanka is divided into **three stages**:

| Stage | Period | Main features |
|---|---|---|
| **Pre-historic era** (Stone Age) | c. 125 000 years ago – at least **1800 B.C.** | Stone tools; hunting and gathering; roaming (nomadic) life; caves and open camps |
| **Proto-historic era** (early Iron Age) | From about **2400 B.C.** | Iron, burnt pots, methodical burials (megalithic), beginning of agriculture and permanent settlement |
| **Early historic era** | **450 B.C. – 300 A.D.** | Village tanks, village settlements, Brahmi inscriptions, early cities |

---

## 2.1 Settlements in the Pre-historic Era

### Evidence and where it was found

| Place | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Rathnapura** and its **gem mines** | Stone tools and bones of extinct animals in the gravel layer (lode) about **90 feet** deep |
| **Iranamadu formation** (semi-arid zone) | Gravel layer with sandy clay soil; Stone Age tools |
| **Pathirajawela** (Hambantota district) | Gravel layer **45 feet** deep – dated **125 000 years** |
| **Bundala, Wellegangoda** | **24 feet** deep – about **80 000 years** |

- The gravel layers came from **erosion of mountains in the extremely rainy Pleistocene era**.
- **Iranamadu Deposit:** laid down during short warm periods of the Pleistocene; a soil belt about **32 km** stretching from the coast inland; named after the **Iranamadu tanks**, where it was first clearly identified.
- **Archaic Homo Sapiens:** the earliest Homo Sapiens, lived between **500 000 and 200 000** years ago.

![Map 2.1 – Distribution of the pre-historic places discovered in Sri Lanka, with six environmental zones](Assets/Chapter_02_Map_01.png)

### Six environmental zones

| Letter | Zone | Example sites |
|---|---|---|
| A | Semi-arid zone | Minihagalkanda, Bundala, Pathirajawela (coastal open camps) |
| B | Lowland dry zone | Pothana and Aligala, Sigiriya (caves) |
| C | Lowland intermediate dry zone | – |
| D | **Wet zone** | Pahiyangala, Batadombalena, Kithulgala Beli Lena (caves); Bellanbendipelessa (open camp) |
| E | Mountainous dry intermediate zone | Bandarawela, Horton Plains (open hunting places) |
| F | Arid zone | Northern region |

> ⭐ Zones are decided by **differences in annual rainfall**. Rainfall affects animals and plants, so food and technology differed. Wet-zone people ate many **land snails**; in the dry zone they hunted **deer and iguana**.

### Key features of settlements
- People lived where **food resources, water and suitable stones** were plentiful (rain forests, dry-zone forests, coastal lagoons, hill-country grasslands).
- **Open areas in the dry season; natural caves in the rainy season.**

### Life style
- Nomadic hunter-gatherers in groups of **15–25** (maximum about **50**). A family of five needed about **50 m²**.
- **Church Hill, Bandarawela:** 150 m², about 25 people. **Bellanbendipelessa:** 120 m², **30 skeletons**.
- Walked about **7 km a day** searching for food.
- **Burial:** buried relatives in the cave where they lived; sometimes dug a pit with a horn or stick. **12 skeletons** at Kithulgala Belilena, **30** at Bellanbendipelessa, **9** at Pahiyangala.
- Skeletons were sometimes **exhumed, painted with red ochre** and reburied.

![Illustration 2.2 – Burial in the cave where the person had lived; later the skeleton was coloured with red ochre](Assets/Chapter_02_Illustration_02.png)

### Food
- **Hunted:** gaur, buffalo, black bear, wild boar, deer, spotted deer, porcupine, hedgehog, hare, stag, giant squirrel, mongoose, wild fowl, monkey, civet cat, iguana; **fresh-water fish** and small fish in natural pools.
- **Plant food (starch):** wild breadfruit, wild plantains, yams (*Gonala, Katuala*), **kithul** seeds, *Dothalu* kernel. **Belilena** has wild breadfruit seeds **baked and eaten 12 500 years ago**.
- Ate **tree-snails** and lagoon **oysters**; **salt** was brought from the coast to inland people (Kithulgala Belilena oysters mixed with salt).

![Fig 2.3 – A bead made from an animal bone, found in a pre-historic habitation](Assets/Chapter_02_Figure_03.png)

### Technology
- **Geometric micro stone tools** – not more than **4.5 cm** (1–7 cm in the textbook definition); made mostly of **quartz** and some **'Kahanda'** stone (alabaster, "Bim Weeduru"); attached to a wooden or bone handle (hafting) for cutting, scratching, chopping and digging. Hammers and grinding stones were made of **granite**.

![Fig 2.2 – Geometric stone tools of the Middle Stone Age made of alabaster](Assets/Chapter_02_Figure_02.png)

![Illustration 2.1 – How micro stone tools were attached to wood or bone](Assets/Chapter_02_Illustration_01.png)

### Physical body
- Adult male ≈ **174 cm**, female ≈ **166 cm**; big teeth; wide nose and chin; brain **1600 cm³** (male), **920 cm³** (female); lifespan about **35–40 years**.

![Fig 2.4 – Pre-historic human skeleton from Pothana cave, near Sigiriya](Assets/Chapter_02_Figure_04.png)

### Rituals
- **Rawana Ella cave** (near Badulla): skull with rough edges rubbed and one side painted with red ochre – clear evidence of ritual.
- Pahiyangala: skeleton parts painted with red ochre. Batadomba Lena (Kuruwita): skeleton **curled and buried**.
- Researchers note **cultural and biological similarities** with the aboriginal (Vedda) community.

## 2.2 Settlements in the Proto-historic Era

- The period **between the end of the pre-historic and the start of the historic era**; technologically a **transition**.
- Features: **burnt pots, methodical burial, iron instead of stone, permanent settlements, agriculture**. By its end, stone tools were completely given up. Also called the **proto-historic Iron Age / early Iron Age**.
- **Cause of change:** climatic change in South Asia between **7000–4500 B.C.** (**Holocene**) – the Ice Age ended, temperatures rose, an environment suited for agriculture was created, population grew.
- Oldest proof: **2400 B.C.**; clearer evidence from the **north-east of Sabaragamuwa** and **south-west of Uva** (Rathnapura – Ranchamadama, Udaranchamadama; Beragala, Haldummulla).

![Map 2.2 – Distribution of megalithic burial grounds of the proto-historic era](Assets/Chapter_02_Map_02.png)

### Megalithic burials
- **Cist burials:** square stone-splinter chambers covered with huge slabs; pots with human remains inside.
- Excavated sites: **Ibbankatuwa** (near Dambulla), **Galsohonkanaththa** (Yapahuwa), **Yatigalpoththa** (near Galewela) – dated **750–450 B.C.**
- **Urn burials:** remains in a clay urn.
- **Beragala and Haldummulla (Uva):** clay pots placed in a **boat-shaped raw-clay tub**; skeletons were cremated in the tub, which baked it hard.

![Illustration 2.3 – Clay urns with cremated remains placed in boat-shaped burial tubs](Assets/Chapter_02_Illustration_03.png)

![Fig 2.5 – A pre-historic cemetery found at Kolambageara Ranchamadama school (Rathnapura), excavated 2007, used about 3350 years ago](Assets/Chapter_02_Figure_05.png)

![Fig 2.6 – Clay tub burial at the proto-historic burial ground, Ranchamadama](Assets/Chapter_02_Figure_06.png)

![Fig 2.7 – A stone casket (cist) tomb from the Ibbankatuwa burial ground near Dambulla](Assets/Chapter_02_Figure_07.png)

### Nature of settlements – sedentism
- **Udaranchamadama house** (Rathnapura, found **2009**): rectangular, **10.20 m × 6.80 m**, wattle-and-daub walls, roof thatched with *Iluk*; about **3000 years old (c. 1129 B.C.**).
- Finds: **painted potsherds** (white base with red lines drawn symmetrically – made on a **potter's wheel**); stone and **iron tools**; bones of deer, cattle, wild boar, giant squirrel (agriculture **plus hunting**); clay beads; an **iron kohl stick**; ochre stones; two grinding stones – women cared about **beautifying** the body.

![Illustration 2.4 – Reconstruction of the Udaranchamadama house (1129 B.C.)](Assets/Chapter_02_Illustration_04.png)

![Fig 2.7(b) – The painted clay pot from Udaranchamadama (1129 B.C.) and its early shape](Assets/Chapter_02_Figure_08.png)

### Technology
- Iron made clearing forests, digging and ploughing easy. Pottery, bead making and house-building (foundations, levelled walls) show advanced technology.

## 2.3 Settlements in the Early Historic Era (450 B.C. – 300 A.D.)

- Thousands of **small village tanks** show where settlements spread; most settlements stood close to a tank, at one end of the **bund**.

![Fig 2.8 – Pits for pillars of a rectangular hut foundation, old interior city of Anuradhapura](Assets/Chapter_02_Figure_09.png)

![Fig 2.9 – Pits for posts of a circular hut foundation, Anuradhapura](Assets/Chapter_02_Figure_10.png)

### Factors that influenced the spread of settlements
- **Geophysical background:** rainfall, soil, geo-diversity, natural resources, drainage.
- Settlements spread in the **dry zone** (rainfall **1000–1250 mm**; **arid zone** below 1000 mm) because of the **fertile red-brown soil**.
- Water came from **monsoon rain** (major) and rivers. River use needed **anicuts and canals** (labour, time, technology), so tanks were built.
- Early settlements grew **outside river valleys** because there was **no technology to control annual floods**. Flood control by anicuts began about the **end of the 1st century B.C. or 1st century A.D.** – e.g. **King Ilanaga (33–43 A.D.)** built an anicut across the **Kirindi river** (Tissamaharama tank). **'Avarana'** (= anicut) appears in Brahmi inscriptions at **Ganekanda** and **Gallena** temples, Kurunegala district.

![Map 2.4 – Ancient settlements around Kirindi Oya, Tissamaharama (900–500 B.C. away from the river; later nearer after flood control)](Assets/Chapter_02_Map_04.png)

### Structure and nature of settlements
- **Vapigama** = village developed around a tank (Mahawamsa: Sumana-, Vihara-, Hundiri-, Kadaha-vapigama). Villages were separated by **jungle belts**.

| Village type | Who lived there |
|---|---|
| Kasikaragama | Farmers |
| Manikaragama | Gem-mining people |
| Gopalagama | Cowherds |
| Kewattagama | Fishermen |
| Kumbhakaragama | Potters |
| Vadtakigama | Carpenters |
| Pattanagama | Near harbours |
| Niyamgam | Trading villages |
| Olagam | Around tanks deserted by people |

- **Sizes:** small village ≤ **0.15 ha**; medium ≈ **2 ha**; large urban > 2 ha. Normally **5–6 families**; large villages ~30 families. By the **6th century A.D.** about **35 000** people in Magama city and adjacent settlements.
- **Parikkita gam** = fenced (protected) village; **Aparikkita gam** = unprotected. **Pura** = large urban settlement; **Pura Kamatha** (250 B.C.) = chief architect; Vijayabahu I's Panakaduwa plate calls Anuradhapura **Nurupura**. **Nakara/Nagara** = settlement between village and city.

### Administration
- **Gahapathi** = chief householder. **Gamika / Gramika** = village chief. **Dasa Gam Eththan** = panel of **ten chief householders** for ten villages (Kaludiya Pokuna inscription, Dambulla).
- Houses: wattle-and-daub with leaf thatch, **round or square/rectangular**; **Walagampaththuwa (Tissamaharama)** house of the **3rd century A.D.**

![Illustration 2.5 – Imaginary drawing of an ancient Sri Lankan village](Assets/Chapter_02_Illustration_05.png)

### Means of subsistence
- **Chena cultivation:** *Kurakkan, Meneri, sesame, Thanahal* – drought-tolerant crops with short roots.
- Growing population → more food → more water → **village tanks**.

### ⭐ Comparison – three eras

| Feature | Pre-historic | Proto-historic | Early historic |
|---|---|---|---|

### ⭐ Important facts
1. First settlements were founded by **Archaic Homo Sapiens** hundreds of thousands of years ago; they showed **no racial identity**.
2. Stone tool technology = pre-historic era (also called the **Middle ("Mediaeval") Stone Age**).
3. Farming was established by about **2400 B.C.**
4. Pots, burial grounds and iron began in the proto-historic era – **basics of Sri Lankan civilization**; people began village life.
5. Mahawamsa describes a well-organised society when **Prince Vijaya** arrived.

# Chapter 03 – Evolution of Political Power in Sri Lanka
*Textbook: Unit 3 (printed pp. 31–43)*

## 0. Introduction
- Sources say Sri Lanka was ruled by many kings and a few queens. **Prince Vijaya** (from India, 6th century B.C.) is named as the first king.
- Archaeology shows settlements **before** Vijaya. Burial grounds at **Beragala and Haldummulla** show an agricultural society developing from **2400 B.C.** – such a society needed some ruling system.
- A ruler needs the **consent of the people**. In the East the word **"Mahasammatha" (general will)** was used for such a king. Rulers without people's consent could not rule long.
- **State** = the territory ruled by a king/queen on the people's will (*Jana sammathaya*) + officials + people.

## 3.1 The Pre-State Era
- No single definite ruler. **Wealthy chieftains** dominated small areas. Early inscriptions call them **Parumaka** (from Sanskrit *Pramukha* = "main").
- Their power came from their wealth.
- **Kothgalakanda inscription (Kandalama, near Dambulla, 250 B.C.)** – a Parumaka offered a cave to monks and is called **"Thota Bojaka"** = one who enjoys/collects taxes of the port (*theertha*). "Anubhava kirima" = collecting taxes and enjoying property – only a ruler could do this.

![Fig 3.1 – Ibbankatuwa burial ground, Dambulla (700–450 B.C.), used by influential people such as Parumakas](Assets/Chapter_03_Figure_01.png)

### Village society (the base of political power)
| Term | Meaning | Key evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Kula** | Family | Villages had about 1–30 families (settled by 900 B.C.) |
| **Gruhapathi / Gahapathi** | Householder; chief of a family | Periyapuliyankulama inscription – householder in the metal trade offered a cave to monks |
| **Gamika (Gramika)** | Chief of a village, chosen from among householders (probably the wealthiest); represented the village in marriages, exchange of goods, disputes; managed the tank and water distribution | Jathaka Atthakatha story of a poor man borrowing a bag of paddy from the Gamika; Samanthapasadika (Buddhaghosha) says Gamika was appointed from householders |
| **Parumaka** | Most powerful Gamika representing all villages of a **tank system** | Hundreds of Parumakas named in inscriptions; women = **Parumakalu** |

![Illustration 3.1 – A village Gamika, one of the householders, who coordinated activities between villages](Assets/Chapter_03_Illustration_01.png)

- Villages were separated by belts of jungle, so people thought of "our village", "neighbouring village" and "outside villages".
- **Evidence of cooperating Gamikas:** a cave at **Korawakgala, Sithulpawwa (Hambantota)** was cleaned and offered by **Gamika Siva, Gamika Sumana and Gamika Thidana** – chiefs of neighbouring villages.

### Origin of Parumakas – the tank system (*Ellangawa*)
1. About 900 B.C. settlements spread in the dry zone around **small rain-fed tanks** (enough for 10–15 families for a short time).
2. Dry seasons and population growth required more water and more cultivated land.
3. Villagers **connected neighbouring tanks by canals** – a **"prolapsed tank" system (Ellangawa)**.
4. Two purposes: (i) conserve water by carrying overflow from an upper tank to a lower one; (ii) keep the land beside the canal damp for cultivation.
5. Connecting tanks needed decisions by many villages → the most powerful Gamika became **Parumaka** for the whole system.
6. Result: **decentralized ruling** – political power started as a divided system.

- Some Parumakas called themselves **king**: **Pochani Raja** ("king of the eastern region", Embul Ambe, Matale) and **Duhathara Raja** (Yatahalena, Kegalle). "King" = one who makes others happy.

## Growth of Kingship (centralization)
- Centralizing power = uniting the country under one person; this creates the **concept of the state**.
- **Pandukabhaya** – first king according to the Mahawamsa: defeated his uncles (provincial rulers), made **Anuradhapura** the capital, and marked village boundaries in his 10th regnal year.
- Reasons for centralization (by **Devanampiyatissa's** time): growth of **Indian Ocean trade**; Sri Lanka's position mid-way on the east–west sea route; gems, elephants and spices attracted traders; an independent country was needed to join this trade.
- Devanampiyatissa: second coronation with the **five royal insignia (Pancha Kakudha Bhanda)** sent by **Emperor Ashoka**; used the title **Devanampiya**; invited Kshatriyas of Kataragama and Chandanagama to the Sri Maha Bodhi planting and planted saplings in many places (building goodwill with regional rulers); Mihindu Thero brought Buddhism.
- **Dutugemunu** united the Parumakas. Generals **Velusumana, Pussadeva, Theraputtabhaya, Nandimitra** appear as *Parumaka* in inscriptions (Sithulpawwa caves) and later as *Senapathi Parumaka*. Other Parumakas became treasurers, cashiers, storekeepers.
- **Bhatikabhaya** – a Sri Lankan envoy visited Rome and returned with glass beads; he performed a great pooja at Ruwanweliseya (statue there).
- **Vasabha** – completed centralization; ruled as one unit; inscriptions across the country; administrative districts (*asthana*) with ministers. The **Vallipuram golden plate** records Jaffna (then **Nagadeepa / Nakadiva**) ruled by minister **Srishigiri**.

## 3.2 The Concept of State
**Duties of rulers:** develop the economy, protect the people, enhance spirituality. Even foreign-origin kings (**Elara, Nissankamalla**) won trust by doing so.

### Concepts of kingship
| Concept | Example |
|---|---|
| **Devathwa** (god) | **Kutakanna Abhaya** called himself *Nareshvara* (Dambulla rock inscription); **Mahasen** worshipped as *God of Minneriya* (dry-zone devalas) |
| **Kuwera** | **Kashyapa I** – *Alakapaya Maha Raja*; Alakamanda = Kuwera's city (said to be a huge rock = Sigiriya) |
| **Parvatharaja** (mountain-king) | Asian rulers' tradition |
| **Bodhisattva** | King shown as responsible, just ruler; people trust and respect him |
| **Chakravarthi** (Sakvithi) – world ruler | Used in 8th–9th centuries A.D.; **Nissankamalla**: "Kalinga Chakravarthi Swaminwahanse" |
| **Veerathwa** (heroism) | – |
- All concepts showed the king's superiority and made people expect exemplary conduct.

### Inheritance of kingship
- Brother to brother (the **eldest younger brother**), or father to son (**most frequent**); the eldest son of the king or of the chief consort was heir.
- Examples: Devanampiyatissa → brothers; Dutugemunu → brother Saddhatissa; Kavantissa → Dutugemunu; Vasabha → Vankanasikatissa.
- Conflict when rules were broken: **Kashyapa** seized the throne from his father **Dhatusena** – Mugalan (son of the chief consort) was the rightful heir; Kashyapa's mother was a second queen.
- Otherwise the throne went to the consort's brother or the king's sister's son.

### Statecraft
- Many Parumakas united; the king became **Mahaparumaka / Mapurumuka**; later **Maha Raja**.

## 3.3 Great Kings
### Dutugemunu
- Saved **Mahatota (Mathota) international harbour**, rich from Indian Ocean trade, from **Elara** who had seized Anuradhapura; planned with father **Kavantissa**.
- Led the **first organized fighting movement** in the country's history; declared he fought for Buddhism, not for royal comforts.
- After victory ordered respect for Elara's tomb (**statesmanship**).
- Combined the Parumakas → became **Maha Raja**.
- During the construction of **Ruwanweli Stupa**: alms halls (*Dansal*) at four gates, **payment to all workers**.

### Vasabha (first ruler of the **Lambakarna** dynasty)
- Inscriptions across the country; divided country into provinces; systematic **tax collection**.
- Built **16 large tanks** (first large-scale tank builder) to meet grain demand of South India (famine in the **Krishna valley**).
- Restored temples and stupas; supported monks.

### Vijayabahu I
- Drove out the **Cholas** after a long period of Chola rule; childhood name **Keerthi**, grew up in **Ruhuna**; protected by security officer **Sithnarubima Budhal Na** (Panakaduwa copper plate).
- Protected **territorial integrity**; restored temples neglected under the Cholas.
- Moved the capital from Anuradhapura to **Polonnaruwa**: trade had moved from West India to **East India**; **Trincomalee harbour (Gokanna Thiththa)** faced the east and could be controlled from Polonnaruwa.

## ⭐ Important Facts
- Political power in Sri Lanka began **decentralized**: Gamika (village) → Parumaka (tank system) → Maha Raja (state).
- **Dutugemunu** began centralization; **Vasabha** (Lambakarna) completed it.
- **State** = king + officials + laws + institutions that apply them.
- **Pancha Kakudha Bhanda**: Walvidunava (fan), Mirivedi Sangala (slippers), Magul Kaduva (sword), Sesatha (parasol), Nalal Patiya (forehead band). Devanampiyatissa's second coronation with items from Ashoka.
- **Devanampiya** = "dear to the gods" (Ashoka's title, used by Tissa).
- **Alakamandava** – abode of Kuwera in the island Uthurukuru.

# Chapter 04 – The Ancient Society of Sri Lanka
*Textbook: Unit 4 (printed pp. 44–62)*

## 0. Introduction
Most sources describe rulers, officials and religious leaders (a minority). To understand the past we must also learn about the **general public** who formed the majority.

## 4.1 The Nature of Ruling
- Three institutions of government: **legislature** (makes laws), **executive** (implements laws – government officers) and **judiciary** (gives justice). In ancient Sri Lanka the executive also acted as the legislature (monarchy) and a judiciary existed.
- **Ekthensamiya** (9th c. A.D. inscriptions) = *Eka asthana* (king's court) + *Samya* (agreement) → "agreement of the king's court". The **Buddhannehela inscription**: land offered with the agreement of the king's court and the king's decree (*Wath himiyan wadala*).
- **Sabhawa (council)** – the judiciary. Officers named Udaya (Meningamuwa) and Sena (Nikawella) came on behalf of the council to give the land **Kerelegama** to **Mahindarama nunnery** (they marked boundaries and recorded conditions).
- **Mahale / Maha Lekhaka** = general secretary who documented government affairs. **Medirigiriya inscription**: Mahale Kashyapa gave land to **Padhanaghara Eth Wehera** by decree of **King Sena II**.
- **Provincial administration** (Anuradhapura could not control distant areas): **Vallipuram golden plate** (Vasabha) – minister **Rishigiri/Srishigiri** governed **Nagadeepa** and built **Piyangukatissa** temple.
- **Dasagam Ettan** – local councils (9th c.) representing ten villages; **Kaludiya Pokuna inscription (Matale)** – they solve problems over alms to the temple.

## 4.2 Economy
### 4.2.1 Agriculture and animal husbandry
**Chena (Hena) cultivation** – the older form. Ancient names: *Sehen* (Buthsarana), *Sen* (Ummagga Jathaka), *Pitibim* (inscriptions).
- Method: choose jungle plot → cut small trees/undergrowth, dry and **burn**; big trees left; owner shouts to chase animals away.
- **Nawa Deli Hena** – newly burnt, fertile chena (also nine crops sprouting: kurakkan, kollu, undu, mun, iringu, thanahal, asamodagam, amu...). **Kanaththa** – re-opened old chena (Anuradhapura).
- A chena was used once or twice and abandoned → forest regrew (no environmental harm).
- Crops: undu, ma, mun, kurakkan, iringu, sesame, amu, mustard (aba), dill (duru), thanahal; vegetables (karabatu, thiththabatu, vambatu, alu puhul, wattakka). Separate plots: *al vee*, yams, **sugar cane (uk)**, **cotton (kapu)**. Jaggery and cloth were produced locally; sugar was not imported until the 16th c.
- **Dandu Weta** – fence; **Pela** – hut at the top of a tall tree for watching at night.
- **Taxes:** *Kethi Ada* and *Ketu Kanaba Aya* – abolished by **Nissankamalla (1187–1196)**.

![Fig 4.2 – A newly prepared chena (Nawadeli Hena)](Assets/Chapter_04_Figure_02.png)

![Illustration 4.1 – A chena with its watch hut and fence](Assets/Chapter_04_Illustration_01.png)

**Paddy cultivation** – to feed a growing population.
- *Kumbura* (small field) and *Ketha* (from Sanskrit *Kshetra*, large fields). Irrigated by rain stored in tanks and carried by canals.
- Seasons (*Kanna*): **Maha Kanna**, **Yala Kanna**, and **Meda Kanna** (between them, depending on water).
- Two water strategies: storing rain (tanks) and diverting rivers with barriers (anicuts). **Parakramabahu I (1153–1186):** "Not a single drop of water that falls from the sky should be sent to the sea without being used."

**Animal husbandry** – cattle rearing (Gopalagam), milk (*mee kiri*), ghee (*githel*), butter (*wendaru*). **Kirigeri** – dairy cattle (Kashyapa IV inscription, Colombo Museum). Rock carving of a woman milking a cow at **Nagapabba, Sigiriya (Mahameru)**. Chickens and goats were reared; the **Medirigiriya hospital inscription** says only the flesh of naturally dead chicken/goats was to be used for patients.

- **Anthewasika / Saddi Viharika** – pupils; **Thera** – senior monks; **Bhanakas** – memorized Tripitaka (Deega Bhanaka, Sanyuktha Bhanaka…) until it was written down in **Valagamba's** time.
- **Vas Visima** (rainy-season retreat) and **Katina Uthsava** (robe offering): Helambagala and Kothalakimbiyawa inscriptions (2200 years old).

## ⭐ Important Facts
- Government = executive + legislature + judiciary; king's court decided by discussion.

# Chapter 05 – The Ancient Science and Technology in Sri Lanka
*Textbook: Unit 5 (printed pp. 63–76)*

## 0. Introduction
- **Science** = understanding and interpreting the elements that affect nature; **technology** = putting those fundamentals into practical use.
- Ancient Sri Lankans applied scientific ideas in daily life (tombs, irrigation, buildings).

## 5.1 Early Stages of Scientific Ideas
- **28 000 years ago**: symmetrical stone tools made of alabaster ("ground-glass" stone) using **pressure flaking** – shows organized technology.
- Applied science began in the pre-historic age and matured in the proto-historic age.

### Clay-tub burials
- Boat- or basin-shaped tombs of raw clay, walls built **perpendicular** to the ground (as at the **Udaranchamadama** house) – reduces pressure on walls; masons today use a plumb line.
- Raw clay burnt well when the skeleton was cremated; in a tomb of **1350 B.C.** a **clay tube** was fixed to supply **air (oxygen)**. Modern science only proposed oxygen's role in burning in the 18th century – Sri Lankans knew it **3250 years ago**.

### House-building
- Pre-historic: stone parapet at **Kithulgala Belilena** (probably held a leaf cover); no clear huts at Bellanbendipelessa.
- Proto-historic: **Udaranchamadama house (1129 B.C., Rathnapura)** – floor of stones and clay, wattle-and-daub wall, internal dividing wall, wooden doorframe post-holes.

### Pottery
- **Sakaporuwa (potter's wheel)**.
- **Black-and-red ware (Kalarakta Varna Meti Bandun):** pot filled with chaff and fired upside down → red outside, black inside (little oxygen inside). This technology disappeared after **300 B.C.**
- Painted pot at Udaranchamadama: white background with orange/red lines using natural red-brown stone pigment; clay was settled in water to get fine particles (like modern porcelain-making).

## 5.2 Maturity of Scientific Usage
### 5.2.1 Water management
**Large tanks (Anuradhapura era):** Kawdulla (2537 ha), Minneriya (2550 ha), Hurulu Wewa (2125 ha), Kala Wewa (2125 ha), Padaviya (2357 ha). Aims: farming, flood control, urban water.

| Component | Function |
|---|---|
| **Tank bund** | Built on natural rock between two hills; layers of clay, soil, gravel and clay (*kirimeti*) beaten down (examined at **Sigiri Mahawewa**; elephants beat the foundation of Ruwanweliseya). **Yodha Wewa bund** ≈ 7 miles long, 44 ft high |
| **Ralapanawa** | Stones on the inner slope to protect the bund from wave erosion |
| **Sorowwa (sluice)** | Stone structure through the bund, releasing water; Wessagiriya inscription of **Mahinda IV** – *Mohol Naga* |
| **Diyaketa Pahana** | Vertical stone slab moved to release water (four *riyan* high at Wessagiriya) |
| **Bisokotuwa** | Square stone chamber (valve pit) reducing pressure; several in high bunds |
| **Mud sluice** | At bottom of bund, to remove silt after the rains |
| **Canals** | *Adika* – officer who built canals (2nd c. B.C.); **Elahera canal** (from Amban Ganga, feeding Minneriya, Giritale, Kawudulla, Kantale); **Minipe canal** from Mahaweli to Polonnaruwa |
| **Ponds** | Kuttam Pokuna, Kumara Pokuna, Eth Pokuna, Nelum Pokuna |
| **Uraketa Lin** | Wells lined with large clay pots for drinking water (dry zone with brackish water) |

**Sigiriya water gardens**
- Four fountains on each side of the western entrance avenues. Water from about **600 ft** height flows through gutters; pressure sends water up; **hole diameter** changes the height (small hole → higher jet).
- Spiral gutters make water flow a long way on a low slope so silt settles; stone splinters remove silt. Fountains still work in heavy rain.
- The western part = *Kshudra Jala Udyanaya* ("minor water park"), symmetrical plan.

### 5.2.2 Architecture

### 5.2.4 Indigenous knowledge – measures
**Area measures:** 1 Karisa = 4 Amunas; 1 Amuna ≈ 2 acres; inscriptions: Bhatikabhaya (Dunumadalakanda) gave **1 Karisa** to a stupa; Kutakanna Abhaya (Horiwila) gave **8 Karisa** to Tissa temple. Cycle: 40 Laha = 1 Pela; 12 Pela = 1 Kuruni (12 Kuruni…); 4 Kuruni = 1 Amuna (text cycle); 1 Amuna = ¼ Karisa.
**Length:** smallest = atom (Paramanu); **36 Paramanu = 1 Anu; 36 Anu = 1 Thajjari; 36 Thajjari = 1 Ratharenu; 36 Ratharenu = 1 Likkha; 7 Likkha = 1 Ukha; 7 Ukha = 1 Dhannamasa; 7 Dhannamasa = 1 Angula; 12 Angula = 1 Viyath (span, 9 inches)**. Long distances: **Gawwa, Yoduna, Usaba**. **Nissankamalla** set up **Gawuthakanu** (distance posts, like km posts) – found at Katugahagalge and Weligatta (Southern Province).
**Weight:** 4 Veeha = 1 Gunja; 2 Gunja = 1 Masaka; 2.5 Masaka = 1 Aka; 8 Aka = 1 Dharana; 5 Dharana = 1 Swarna; 2 Swarna = 1 Pala. **Soraborawewa pillar inscription** forbids unapproved weights and scales.
**Numerals:** ancient numerals 1–10 in inscriptions (Chart 5.1); Arabic numerals are modern.
**Time:** *Varshaya* (lunar months); 30 days = 15 *Purapakshaya* + 15 *Awapakshaya*; months Duruthu, Navam, Medin; year = **Vasa / Havajara** (Sanskrit *Samvatsara* → Havurudu → **Avurudu**); day = **Thithiya** (*Thatha* – Rajagala inscription of King Abhaya, Batticaloa); moment = *Muhurtha* → *Mohotha*.

# Chapter 06 – Historical Knowledge and Its Practical Application
*Textbook: Unit 6 (printed pp. 77–89)*

## 0. Introduction
- Knowledge systems differ among societies but support their sustainability; knowledge passed on is **cultural heritage**.
- Six concepts studied: **social organization, law and tradition, finance and exchange, representation of women, local food, protecting the environment**.

## 6.1 Social Organization in the Historical Era
- **Hierarchy** – *vertical* (king → ministers → officials) and *horizontal* (ministers, generals, treasurers – equal in power).
- **Social structure (Chart 6.1, Prof. Senaka Bandaranayake, 2013)**

| Level | Group |
|---|---|
| Top | **King** (noble; virtuous and of royal lineage – e.g., *Nara Isera* of Kutakanna Tissa, Dambulla) |
| Royal officers | **Radkol Samdaru** ("Raja Kula Swami Daru"), later **Radala** (Rajakula > Radkol > Radol > Radala) in the Kandyan kingdom |
| Clergy | Buddhist monks – advisers; in the 1st–2nd c. A.D. temples were given the right to collect tank taxes |
| Chieftains | Rich merchants, naval captains, doctors, teachers (not farmers/herders) |
| Common people – **Kudeen** | farmers, herdsmen, potters, gem-cutters, blacksmiths, fishermen |
| Bottom | **Slaves**: *Anwayathadasa* (hereditary) and *Dhanankeethadasa* (bought) |
- Traders and government officers acted as **mediators** between people and rulers.
- **Social cohesion** was protected by the king maintaining bonds among all groups.

## 6.2 Law and Tradition
- Principles of society are **written laws** or **traditions**.
- **Manawamma** dynasty (9th c. A.D.) inscriptions: **Wadala** (legislature – king's court) and **Sabhawa** (judiciary).
- Security officers: **Mekapper** (from Tamil *Mayikappar*); **Dandanayaka** (e.g., **Sithnarubima Buddhanayake** who protected Vijayabahu I – Panakaduwa plate).

### Tax law (1st–2nd c. A.D. inscriptions)
| Tax | Sanskrit | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Dakapathi** | Udakaprapthi | Tank-water use |
| **Bojakapathi** | Bhojakaprapthi | Cultivating royal land |
| **Mataramajibaka** | Mathrikamathsyabhaga | Fishing in water channels |
- **One-sixth** of produce was paid as tax; one nut in ten (9th c.). **Customs** collected at harbours – **Godawaya inscription (1st c. A.D., Hambantota)** gave a nearby fort's customs to the temple.
- **Courts:** Gajabahu I's **Sithulpawwa inscription** – fines of the *Mahavinichaya* (Great Hall of Justice) given to monks.

### Laws protecting agriculture and trade
- **Kondawattawan inscription (9th c.):** fine of **five Kalan** for not ploughing at the right time (prevents soil erosion).
- **Wessagiriya inscription of Mahinda IV:** only **Sihineti** paddy and green gram (mun) to be sown in paddy fields.
- **Sorabora Wewa (Badulla) inscription of Udaya III (946–954):** laws for the **Hopitigama market** – tax from traders but not from passers-by; double tax for hiding trade; no unapproved *Madatiya* weights; areca/betel sold in covered huts; oil tax for trading on poya days.

### Laws protecting life
- **Amandagamini Abhaya (19–29 A.D.)** – "Maghatha" law: no killing of animals.
- **Mihindu IV (956–972)** – Mihintale inscription: death for killing buffalo, ox, cow, goat; **Wewalkaetiya** inscription: death by hanging for murder; cattle thieves branded on forehead; **Gruhadanda** – villagers taxed until they caught criminals.
- **Nissankamalla (1187–1196)** – birds in reservoirs not to be killed.
- **Roman-Dutch law** came much later.

## 6.3 Finance and Exchange
- First **barter**; clay discs may have been used as tokens.
- **Coins from c. 250 B.C.** – **Hasebu coins** (silver punch-marked, Indian origin, round/square/rectangular) = **Kahapana** (inscriptions)/**Kahavanu** (literature), from Sanskrit *Karshapana*.

- Foreign coins: Roman (most numerous, mostly copper), Chinese, Arabic, British, **V.O.C.** (Dutch East India Company). Customs paid in coins weighed by metal.

## 6.5 Local Food
- Pre-historic: wild breadfruit and *Atikesel* at **Beligala (Belilena) cave 10 500 years ago**.

- Fish/meat eaten; **Mathrika Mathsya Bhagaya** was the fish tax.
- People preferred natural, unprocessed foods.

# Chapter 07 – Decline of Ancient Cities in the Dry Zone and Origin of New Kingdoms in the South West
*Textbook: Unit 7 (printed pp. 91–105)*

## 0. Introduction
- Anuradhapura (North Central dry zone) and Magama (Southern dry zone) were great cities for a long time. Anuradhagrama became a city under **Pandukabhaya** (Mahawamsa).
- Urbanization is a long gradual process driven by internal economic growth and foreign trade.
- **Urbanization** – a village turning into a city. Village: small, kin-based. City: large population linked by professions, trade, craftsmanship, a ruling structure and a script. **Social stratification** – differences by wealth (working class, middle class, capitalist class).

## 7.1 Background of Urban Life
- Basic urban features at Anuradhapura and Magama by **c. 450 B.C.**
- Evidence from **2400 B.C.** that people settled and farmed → villages.
- Differences in soil fertility and skill created **surplus (Athirikthaya)** for some farmers.
- **Jataka Atthakatha story:** a villager borrowed a bag of paddy from the village leader, who was rich through surplus. The leader's power came from wealth – like **Parumaka** and **Batha** in inscriptions. Rich/poor division = **origin of social stratification**.

## 7.2 The First Urbanization
- By **450 B.C.** social stratification was largely complete; many surplus-owning chieftains (Parumakas) used **imported goods** (pottery, beads found at Anuradhapura and Tissamaharama) to show prestige.
- Indian Ocean trade brought wealth → need for administration → planned settlement with **markets, religious buildings, parks, moats and tanks = a city**.
- **Anuradhapura** (planned by Pandukabhaya) was capital for about **1400 years**; self-sufficient with village produce, iron industry and irrigation. **Magama** reflected southern culture.
- After the **6th c. A.D.** Arab traders controlled Indian Ocean trade; after the **9th c.** trade centred on the **eastern** Indian Ocean (South-East Asia, South China) → **Vijayabahu I** chose **Polonnaruwa**.
- Under Parakramabahu I South-East Asian influence grew; later kings married royals of **Burma and Cambodia**; South-East Asian features in **Polonnaruwa Gal Vihara Buddha statues** and **Sathmahal Prasada**.

## 7.3 Downfall of Polonnaruwa
- Mihindu V (last Anuradhapura king) fell to Cholas; **Vijayabahu I** freed the country and made Polonnaruwa capital; **Parakramabahu I** reunited it and rebuilt irrigation.
- **Reasons for decline**

| # | Reason | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Weak rulers | **12 rulers in 29 years (1186–1215)**; only **Nissankamalla** ruled 9 years firmly |
| 2 | Pandya–Kalinga struggle | Kalingas brought Chodaganga (nephew), Sahasamalla, Kalyanawathi; Pandyans brought **Queen Leelawathi** to power three times |
| 3 | Economic deterioration | Tanks/anicuts neglected; trade fell |
| 4 | **Invasion of Kalinga Magha, 1215** | Looting, torture, destroyed statues and temples (Mahawamsa); people fled to **Ruhuna, Malaya Rata, Maya Rata** |
- Ruwanweli, Abhayagiriya, Jetavanaramaya went into wilderness.
- **General causes of decline of ancient cities:** invasions, collapse of central rule, insecurity, paralysis of trade and transport, migration.

## 7.4 The Second Urbanization
- Features: several ruling centres, new harbours, literary revival, new trade networks. Cities are smaller and **not geometrically planned**.

### Kingdoms of the South West
| Kingdom (dates) | Rulers and key events |

| **Gampola (from 1341)** | Founded by **Buvanekabahu IV** (1341); Dedigama rival Parakramabahu V; Jaffna (Arya Chakravarthi) and Rayigama (Alakeshwara) kingdoms existed. Succession from **uncle to nephew / sister's son** (matriarchal). Monuments: **Lankathilaka** and **Gadaladeniya** temples (Buvanekabahu IV) |
| **Kotte (1415–)** | **Parakramabahu VI (1412 Rayigama; 1415 Kotte coronation; ruled to 1467)**, protected by **Veedagama Thero**; parents Jayamahalena and Sunethra Devi; brought Wanni under Kotte; **Prince Sapumal** captured Jaffna; suppressed **Count Jothiya** (Uda Rata); patron of literature. Successor **Jayabahu** (grandson via Ulakudaya Devi) killed by Sapumal → **Buvanekabahu VI (1469)**; **Sinhala Sangha rebellion** (Pasyodun Rata Sinhala Peraliya) suppressed by **Prince Ambulugala**; **Abhayadana Shilalipiya (Dedigama inscription)** pardoned rebels. **Parakramabahu VII (Pandita)**; **Veera Parakramabahu VIII (c. 1490)**; **Portuguese arrived in 1505** |

### Jaffna and the Vanniyars
- **Jaffna kingdom** – founded by **Vijaya Kulankeyi Arya Chakravarthi**; texts: *Yalpana Vaipava Maalai* (18th c.), *Kailaya Maalai*, *Segarasasekaram*. Twelve rulers (Kulasekara, Vikrama, Varodaya, Marthanda…). Brought under Kotte by Sapumal; loosened after Parakramabahu VI; surrendered to the Portuguese later.
- **Vanni** – small rulers in low-population areas (East, North Central, North Western); **18 Vanni areas** by Parakramabahu VI's time; Vijayabahu III originally a Vanni leader; under the main kingdoms in strong periods; also under Kandyan kings.

### 7.4.1 Economy
- Wet-zone agriculture relied on rain (no huge tanks); **paddy**, chena in dry places; shift to commercial economy.
- **Exports:** cinnamon (main), areca nut, cardamom, nutmeg, pepper, pearls, gems, elephants, tusks. **Partners:** India, Arabia, China, Persia, Egypt. Trade was a **royal monopoly**. Chinese porcelain at Dedigama; Ibn Battuta on west-coast ports.

### 7.4.2 Religion
- Buddhism main; Tooth Relic palaces. Gods: **Upulvan, Vibhishana, Skanda Kumara, Saman**; Shiva Devala near Sri Jayawardhanapura; **Munneswaram** (Chilaw); Muslim settlements at ports.

### 7.4.3 Arts, architecture and literature
- Yapahuwa – Pandyan and Cambodian influence; **Gadaladeniya** (South Indian style, Upulvan devala attached); **Lankathilaka**; **Embekke Devala** (wood carvings, Vikramabahu III).
- **Dambadeniya literature:** *Kavsilumina* (Parakramabahu II), *Saddharmaratnavaliya* (Dharmasena), *Poojavaliya* (Mayurapada Buddhaputra), *Sidatsangarava*, *Elu Sandes Lakuna*, *Dambadeni Katikavata*, *Kandavuru Siritha*, *Yogarnavaya*; Pali: *Visuddhimagga Sannaya, Samantakuta Varnana, Rasavahini, Sarasangraha, Bhesajjamanjusa, Hatthavanagalla Vihara Vamsa, Thupavamsa*.
- **Kotte literature (golden age):** Sandesha poems (*Mayura, Thisara, Selalihini, Parevi, Hansa, Gira*), *Kavyashekharaya, Buduguṇālaṅkāraya, Loveda Sangarava, Guttila Kavya, Perakumba Siritha*, *Pansiya Panas Jataka Pota*; Pali: *Parami Shathakaya, Jinabodhavali, Balavatara*; Sanskrit: *Bhakti Shatakaya*; scholars **Thotagamuve Sri Rahula, Keragala Vanaratana, Irugalkula Thilaka Pirivena monks**; pirivenas: **Pepiliyana Sunethradevi**, **Keragala Padmavati**, **Devinuvara Irugalkulatilaka**, **Veedagama Gunananda**.

## ⭐ Important Facts

# Chapter 08 – Kandyan Kingdom

After the fall of Kotte and Seethawaka, the **Kandyan kingdom protected the country's independence until 1815**, facing challenges no earlier kingdom had faced.

- Under Parakramabahu VI, Kandy was a provincial unit of Kotte. **Senasammatha Vikramabahu (1469–1511)** made it independent → **founder**.

- **Challenges:** no legal royal inheritance; Portuguese threat; Seethawaka influence; decline of Buddhism; economy.

## 8.2 Administration
- **King** at top; **two Adikarams** (Maha Adikaram = **Pallegampaha**; second = **Udagampaha**); **royal council** with Adikarams, Disawes, Maha Lekam and Badde Lekam.
- **Three divisions:** palace service (**Maha Vasala Sevaya**), provincial rule (**Rata Wasama**), **Badde** (tax/craft structure) and temples/devalas.
- **King's powers:** highest authority; keep peace and protection; follow tradition; **highest court**, could order death; priority in all affairs.
- **Katupulle messengers** – carried Adikarams' orders; symbol silver-ringed cane with bent top.
- **Palace officers:** Gajanayaka Nilame, Maha Lekam, Maha Gabada Nilame, Diyawadana Nilame, Halu Wadana Nilame, Bath Wadana Nilame, Asapanthiye Muhandiram, Kavikara Maduwe Muhandiram.
- **Provinces:** **21 divisions** – 9 **Ratas** near Kandy (Udunuwara, Yatinuwara, Tumpane, Harispattuwa, Dumbara, Hewaheta, Kotmale, Uda Bulatgama, Pata Bulatgama; officer **Rate Rala/Rate Mahatmaya**, under him Liyana Rala, Undiya Rala) and **12 Disawes** (Hathara Korale, Hat Korale, Uva, Sabaragamuwa, Matale, Walapane…; officer **Disawe**). Disawe → **Korale** → **Pattu**; **Muhandirams** (by caste), **Vidanes** (village).
- **Badde structure:** Madige (transport), Kuruwe (elephant catching), Badahela (pottery), Rada (washing), Handa (weaving); later under Disawes.
- **Temples:** Mahanayake and Anunayake Theros; Dalada Maligawa through **Diyawadana Nilame**.
- **Judicial:** king highest; reserved cases – important leaders, temples/monks, riots, treason, conspiracy. **Punishments:** beating, expulsion to Rodiya groups, fines, drowning, Dandu Kanda, cutting hair, whipping, trampling by elephants, thrashing with *Ipala*, imprisonment.

## 8.3 Economy
- Agrarian, consumer-based and self-sufficient; limited paddy land (hills; decline of irrigation); paddy terraces on slopes; much **chena** (kurakkan, amu, maize, wheat, yams, sweet potato); home gardens (jak, breadfruit, pepper, cardamom, areca, fruits); industries – iron, wood, metal, lacquer, reed; animal husbandry; barter.

## 8.4 Social organization
- King owned all land. **Land types:** **Gabadagama** (palace), **Nindagama** (aristocrats for service), **Viharagama**, **Devalagama**, **Paraveni** (villagers' hereditary land).
- Service system: **Rajakariya**.
- Self-sufficient villages; **caste-based** society; temple centre of life.
- **Marriages:** **Deega** (bride goes to groom's house), **Binna** (groom lives in bride's house), **Eka Geyi** (polyandry – one wife for brothers).

## ⭐ Important Facts
- Last kingdom of Sri Lanka; dealt with Portuguese, Dutch and British.
- Nayakkar dynasty (South Indian) from 1739.
- **1815 – Udarata (Kandyan) Convention** signed by Governor **Robert Brownrigg** and Kandyan chieftains → ended the kingdom.

# Chapter 09 – Renaissance
*Textbook: Unit 9 (printed pp. 118–126)*

## 0. Introduction
- **Renaissance = "rebirth"**: the wide transformation in art and science in **Europe from the 14th to 16th centuries A.D.**
- **Pristine (classical) age** – ancient Greek and Roman civilizations. **Mediaeval period** – about 1000 years after Rome fell in the **5th century**. In the late mediaeval era interest in Greek–Roman literature, art and philosophy revived → **modern era** began.

## 9.1 Reasons for the Renaissance
| Reason | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Fall of Constantinople (1453)** | The Turks captured the city where Greek and Roman books and scholars were; scholars fled to other European cities with manuscripts and spread classical learning |
| **Growth of trade and a new rich class** | From the **12th c.** East–West trade enriched **Venice and Genoa**; wealthy people independent of feudal nobles supported art. Italy's small states encouraged patronage. **Medici family of Florence** is a key example |
| **Intellectual revival** | New educational centres (after the 12th c.) taught ancient literature, drama, phonetics and history, not only theology, philosophy and law. **Gutenberg's printing machine (1454)** made books abundant |
| **Criticism of Church dominance** | Worldly priests were criticised by **John Wycliffe (England), John Huss (Bohemia), Erasmus, Martin Luther (Germany)** |

![Fig 9.1 – Painting of two divine children in the Sistine Chapel by Michelangelo](Assets/Chapter_09_Figure_01.png)

## How the Renaissance transformed Europe
### Academic Renaissance (15th c.)
- **Language and literature:** Latin and Greek were revived (Greek was a "dead" language); local tongues declined for a time. **Greek drama** attracted interest. Authors: **Dante** (*Divine Comedy*), **Petrarch** (*Sonnets to Laura*), **Boccaccio** (Italy); **Sir Thomas More, Francis Bacon, Shakespeare** (England); **Erasmus** (France).
- **Humanism:** man regarded as noble; humanistic subjects (literature, history, art, law, ethics) replaced theological ones; critical thinking.

### Arts and crafts
- Mediaeval craftsmen were organised in grades and art was religious (church patronage). Renaissance artists worked independently as individuals under rich patrons; worldly themes, nude figures, portraits of wealthy people.
- **Painting** (Florence centre): **Leonardo da Vinci** (poet, musician, scientist, engineer; *Mona Lisa*, *Last Supper*), **Michelangelo** (Sistine Chapel ceiling, *Last Judgement*, *Creation of Adam*), **Raphael, Masaccio, Botticelli**.
- **Sculpture:** marble/alabaster works – Michelangelo's *Pieta, Moses, David*, tomb of Lorenzo de' Medici.
- **Architecture:** from pointed spires and pyramidal pillars (Gothic) to **round arches, tall columns and half-round domes** (Roman style). Examples: **St Peter's Basilica**, Florence Cathedral. Architects: Michelangelo, Leonardo, Raphael.

![Fig 9.3 – A Renaissance-style residential building in France](Assets/Chapter_09_Figure_03.png)

### Scientific advancement
| Scientist | Contribution |
|---|---|
| **Nicolaus Copernicus** (Polish) | Planets circle the sun ("father of modern astronomy") – (book text states earth round) |
| **Isaac Newton** (England) | Law of gravitation |
| **Johannes Kepler** (German) | Confirmed Copernicus; planetary orbits |
| **Galileo Galilei** (Italy) | Telescope; craters on the moon, moons of Jupiter; "father of experiment" |
| **William Harvey** (England) | Blood circulation |
| **Paracelsus** (Swiss) | Causes and remedies of disease; modern pharmacology |
| **Michael Servetus** (text: "Michael Certes") | Pulmonary circulation of blood |
| **Johannes Gutenberg** (German) | Printing machine |

![Fig 9.2 – Galileo examining the sky with his telescope](Assets/Chapter_09_Figure_02.png)

### Political revival
- Feudal lords → **national states** united under one king; powers centred on the king. A **middle class** (traders, bankers, shipowners, caravan owners) grew and supported the kings; aristocrats lost power.

### Economic revival
- Self-sufficient mediaeval economy declined; **trade grew**; national trade and tax policies; **currency, cheques and banks** developed; loans to businessmen; new sea routes brought spices, gold, silver, pearls, gems, elephants and tusks.

### Discovery of new trade routes
- **1453:** Turks closed trade routes. Helping factors: compass, knowledge that the earth is round, **Ptolemy's world map**, state sponsorship.
- **Portugal and Spain** led. **Prince Henry the Navigator** (Portugal) supported exploration.
- **Bartolomeu Dias** reached the southern cape of Africa, named it **Cape of Storms** (king renamed it **Cape of Good Hope**).
- **Vasco da Gama** reached **Calicut** (1498) – first sea route to Asia.
- **Columbus** (Spain; Ferdinand and Isabella) – West Indies; **Amerigo Vespucci** – America ("New World"); **Magellan** – Philippines; **Cabot** brothers – Canada.

### Global trend
- Began in **Florence**, spread through Italy and Europe. Europeans established **colonies in Asia, Africa and the Americas for about 450 years** from the 16th century; printing, European thought, religion, architecture and science spread worldwide.

## 9.2 Influence of the Renaissance on Sri Lanka
| Field | Effects |
|---|---|
| **Political** | Kandyan kingdom independent until 1815 – little effect earlier; **British rule 1815–1948** ended monarchy; introduced **parliamentary government**, new administrative officers replacing Adikarams/Disawes/Mohottalas/Korales; legal system on European lines (Dutch had introduced **Roman–Dutch law**) |
| **Economic** | Trade centres moved from Mediterranean cities (Venice, Genoa, Constantinople) to Atlantic countries (Portugal, Spain, France, Holland, England). **Dutch** grew cinnamon; **British** coffee, cocoa, cinchona, tea, rubber; self-sufficient village economy collapsed; import–export economy (even food imported); money use, **railways and roads**, cities **Colombo, Galle, Trincomalee**, new middle class |
| **Social and cultural** | **Printing** introduced by Dutch, popular under British (books, magazines, newspapers); students went to Europe; **Reformation (1517)** – Catholic (Portuguese) and Protestant (Dutch) faiths; European education system; church architecture |

## ⭐ Important Facts
- Renaissance began in **Florence, Italy**.
- 1453 (Constantinople), 1454 (printing), 1498 (Vasco da Gama in Calicut), 1517 (religious reformation).
- Renaissance became a global trend through colonialism.

# Chapter 10 – Sri Lanka and the Western World
*Textbook: Unit 10 (printed pp. 127–139)*

## 0. Introduction
Europeans turned to the East from the start of the second millennium A.D. for trade. In Sri Lanka **political motives soon exceeded commercial ones**, so relations were marked by native resistance to foreign domination.

## 10.1 Arrival of the Portuguese in Asia
- **Three trade routes** between Asia and Europe (all linked to **Constantinople**): (1) **Silk Route** – Constantinople → Persia → Central Asia → China; (2) Constantinople → Egypt → Red Sea → Indian Ocean; (3) Constantinople → Baghdad → Persian Gulf → west coast of India.
- Muslims held Asian trade monopoly; when the **Turks captured Constantinople in 1453** prices rose, supplies fell – Europeans wanted cinnamon and pepper and had to find new routes.
- **Portugal** (Iberian Peninsula, facing the Atlantic) led: **Prince Henry the Navigator** ran a naval school (seamanship, mapping, astronomy).
- **Vasco da Gama reached Calicut in 1498** → European domination of Asia (≈ **450 years** from the 16th c.). Dutch, English and French followed.
- **Objectives:** economic gain, spreading religion, international prestige. Portuguese: defeat Muslims, seize trade monopoly and spread **Roman Catholicism**. Da Gama's party: *"We have come in search of Christians and spices."* Second group asked the ruler of Calicut for a warehouse and permission for five priests.

![Fig 10.1 – Dutch symbol carved on an old building in Colombo](Assets/Chapter_10_Figure_01.png)

## 10.2 Political Condition of Sri Lanka at the Portuguese arrival
- Known to the Portuguese as **Seilan**, famous for good cinnamon.
- **March 1505:** Portugal sent **Francisco de Almeida** as viceroy; his son **Lourenço de Almeida** reached Sri Lanka by accident (storm), **1505**.
- Country divided into **Kotte, Kandy (Udarata) and Jaffna**. Proverb **"Parangiya Kotte giya wage"** – Portuguese envoys were led along a winding road to make Kotte seem far.
- **Vijayaba Kollaya (1521):** after Vijayabahu VI, princes **Buvanekabahu, Mayadunne and Rayigam Bandara** (backed by Jayaweera Bandara of Kandy) killed the king (the killer was **Salman**) because the succession favoured prince Devaraja. **Kotte divided**: **Buvanekabahu VII – Kotte; Mayadunne – Seethawaka; Rayigam Bandara – Rayigama.**

![Map 10.1 – Distribution of Dutch fortresses in Sri Lanka](Assets/Chapter_10_Map_01.png)

## Expansion of the Portuguese in the coast
### Kotte
- Buvanekabahu VII vs Mayadunne over succession (grandson **Dharmapala**, son of **Samudra Devi** and **Veediye Bandara**). Buvanekabahu invited the Portuguese; **Muslims backed Mayadunne**, who got naval help from the **Samorin of Calicut** but failed.
- The king resisted conversion; disputes over trade prices; he was **shot by a Portuguese soldier on 29 December 1550** (Queiroz).
- **Dharmapala (1551–1597)** – puppet; became Catholic; **gifted his kingdom to the Portuguese** (deed of gift, so they had legal right on his death); the Tooth Relic was taken to Seethawaka.
- **Veediye Bandara** – rebelled, imprisoned in Colombo fort; his wife dug a tunnel; fought from **Pelenda** (Pas Yodun Korale); defeated by Mayadunne + Portuguese; killed later in Jaffna.

### Seethawaka
- **Mayadunne** anti-Portuguese; son **Tikiri Bandara** led the army (c. 1555); 1557 attack on Kotte failed due to Portuguese artillery.
- **Battle of Mulleriyawa** – about **1600 Portuguese killed**.
- **Dharmapala moved to Colombo fort c. 1565.**
- **c. 1581: Tikiri Bandara became Rajasinghe I**; took Kandy; confined Portuguese to Colombo and a few places by 1582; two sieges failed (1587 – canal from Bere tank); no navy; Goa support for the Portuguese. Died from a **bamboo thorn wound at Pethangoda** after defeat by Vimaladharmasooriya.
- **Collapse causes:** constant wars; anti-Buddhist policy later; killing **Veerasundara Bandara**; **Konappu Bandara** in Kandy; no heir.

### Jaffna
- **Arya Chakravarthi** dynasty; **Pararajasekaram**; Portuguese ignored Jaffna at first (no cinnamon/pepper). **Sankili (1519–1561)** – attacked Portuguese ships; opposed Catholic spread; **Portuguese invaded 1543** (ransom paid); Sankili punished Mannar converts; allied with Mayadunne (1548); sheltered Veediye Bandara; **1560 Portuguese invasion** – **Mannar fort** built and pearl-fishery revenue lost. **Puvirajapandaram (1561)** overthrew Sankili. Portuguese installed **Edirimannasinham**; allowed Senarath to trade with South India. 1617 power struggle → **Sankili Kumara**; **Philip de Oliveira seized Jaffna in 1621**.
- **Reasons for fall:** heirs' disputes, small army, no natural defence.

## Portuguese attempts on Kandy
### Battle of Danture (October 1594)
- Portuguese under **Pero Lopes de Sousa** placed **Kusumasana Devi (Dona Catherina)** as queen; Kandyans backed **Vimaladharmasooriya I**; the entire Portuguese force destroyed; **Pero Lopes killed**; Kusumasana Devi captured and married the king → **new dynasty**. Low-country troops joined the king.

### Battle of Balana (1602)
- After Danture a low-country rebellion under **Edirille Rala** (supported by the king) was crushed. **Don Jeronimo de Azevedo** invaded 1602 with 1000 soldiers and troops from Goa; **trapped at the Balana pass**; retreated via Malwana. Afterwards smaller raids; **Senarath's treaty with the Portuguese in 1617** failed.

### Battle of Randeniwela (July 1630)
- **Constantino de Sá** with about **600 Portuguese + 4500 lascarins** took and burned **Badulla**; Prince **Maha Asthana (Rajasinghe II)** led Kandyans; low-country Mudaliyars returned to the king; Portuguese retreated to Randeniwela near Wellawaya – about **350 killed**, de Sá killed. Planned two years.

### Battle of Gannoruwa (March 1638)
- **Diogo de Melo** with about 1000 Portuguese + thousands of lascarins entered Kandy; Rajasinghe II abandoned the city, then encircled the Portuguese at Gannoruwa; **fewer than 100 Portuguese survived**; de Melo killed; **last Portuguese invasion** of Kandy.

**Reasons for Kandyan success:** people's loyalty to king; low-country troops rejoining the king; warfare and strategy; natural position.

## 10.3 Sri Lanka and the Dutch
- **VOC (Dutch East India Company, founded 1602)**; base **Batavia (Java)**; motive – profit; interest in cinnamon and pepper.
- **1602:** **Joris van Spilbergen** met Vimaladharmasooriya I; **Sebald de Weert** followed; no result.
- **Rajasinghe II** – wanted naval help; with Dutch officer **Westerwold** attacked **Batticaloa fort (1638)** – Portuguese surrendered.

### 1638 Kandy–Dutch Agreement
1. Dutch to help expel the Portuguese.
2. King to repay Dutch costs in trade goods (cinnamon, pepper).
3. Dutch monopoly of Kandyan goods except elephants.
4. Dutch to garrison captured forts only with the king's consent.
- **Forts captured by 1658:** Trincomalee, Negombo, Galle, Kalutara, Colombo, Mannar, Jaffna. The Dutch **kept the advantageous forts**; king failed to make Sri Lanka free; he claimed to be **"Emperor of Sri Lanka"**; Dutch addressed him as **Thrisinhaladheeshwara** nominally.
- **Dutch attacks 1665–1668; Rajasinghe II's counter-attacks 1670–1675**; Dutch avoided war for about 100 years.

![Fig 10.2 – A meeting between Kandyan leaders and the Dutch](Assets/Chapter_10_Figure_02.png)

### Dutch policy
- Peace and gifts; exchange of envoys; supplying ships for Upasampada missions (Vimaladharmasooriya I, Vijaya Rajasinghe, Keerthi Sri Rajasinghe).
- **Economic policy:** isolate the king from other Europeans; close borders in disputes; develop paddy; promote **cinnamon**; grow sugar cane, coffee, cotton.

### Conflicts under Keerthi Sri Rajasinghe
- **1760:** low-country rebellions in **Negombo and Matara**; Kandyan army took Katuwana and Matara forts (later retaken).
- **1764:** Dutch invasion under Governor **Baron van Eck** with 6 brigades failed.
- **1765:** Dutch entered Kandy, stayed over a month, left due to hunger, disease and rain.
- **1766 peace pact:** Kandy lost a coastal area; Dutch rights over their territory recognized; Kandyans never put it fully into effect.

## ⭐ Important Facts
- Reasons for Portuguese success: disunity (Vijayaba Kollaya), Dharmapala's conversion/deed of gift.
- Kandyan victories: **Danture (1594), Balana (1602), Randeniwela (1630), Gannoruwa (1638)**.
- Dutch came through the 1638 treaty; Portuguese expelled by **1658**.
