# Chapter 01 – Industrial Revolution
*Textbook: Chapter 1 (printed pp. 1–18)*

## 0. Introduction
- **Industrial Revolution** = the broad change in industry and technology, over about **six decades**, in which goods were produced by **machines instead of manual labour**.

## 1.1 Beginning and Development
- Began in **Britain in the late 18th century**; confined to Britain for about half a century; spread across Europe and the world in the 19th century.
- Other colonial powers (France, Portugal, Spain) existed – so why Britain?

### Reasons it began in Britain
| Reason | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Wealth and trade | Since the 16th c. Britain overtook Holland, France, Portugal and Spain; a wealthy investor class emerged |
| Population growth | Higher demand for goods |
| Science after the **Renaissance** | Knowledge used to build machines |
| Natural resources | Iron, steel and **coal** (energy) |
| Colonial markets | Commercial laws blocked colonies from trading with others → guaranteed demand (e.g., Indian demand for British **cotton cloth**) |
| Agrarian change | **Encroaching (enclosure) movement** – rich landowners merged small farms into large farms; dispossessed farmers moved to cities as labour |
| Free social environment | Talented people could earn money regardless of status |
| Banks and finance | Capital for industry |
| Political stability | Parliament gave freedom, tax relief and support to business |
| Geography | Small island; network of markets; London as centre for raw materials, capital and products |

## 1.2 Rise of the Industrial Revolution
Three base industries: **textile, iron and steel, coal**.

### Textile industry
| Year | Inventor | Invention |
|---|---|---|
| 1733 | **John Kay** | **Flying shuttle** (faster weaving) |
| 1764 | **James Hargreaves** | **Spinning Jenny** (manual cotton-spinning machine) |
| 1769 | **Richard Arkwright** | **Water frame** (water-powered; large, needed flowing water) |
| c. 1779 | **Samuel Crompton** | **Mule** – finer, stronger thread |
| c. 1785 | **Edmund Cartwright** | **Steam-powered** loom/mule |
- Before this, thread from **six spinners** was needed for **one weaver**.

### Coal industry
- Coal mines: **South Wales, Yorkshire, Lancashire**. Used for smelting, machines and homes.
- Problems: water in deep mines, poisonous gas, heat, darkness.
- **1735 Thomas Newcomen** steam engine (pumping water; inefficient) → **James Watt** improved steam engine (text gives 1736) – efficient for many industries.
- **1812 Humphry Davy** safety lamp; **1839** iron cables replaced women and children hauling coal.
- By-products: **tar** (roads), dyes, medicines, perfumes, oil, fertilizers.

### 2. To other countries
- Causes: rising world population/demand; improved travel and transport; **coal in France and Germany**.
- **Germany** became powerful by the time of World War I; France, Belgium, Austria, Hungary, Prussia followed (Austria built 30 000 miles of roads; Belgium doubled its roads). **USA** developed rapidly after independence; **Japan** in the late 19th–early 20th century (following Western models). Influence reached China, India and Sri Lanka by the 20th century → **"global village"**.

## Results of the Industrial Revolution
### Political
- Rich investors vs. **working class**; unions, boycotts, struggles for rights → **socialism and liberalism**.
- **Adam Smith's *laissez-faire*** (reduced government role) spread **capitalism**.
- Competition for cheap raw materials and markets → **neo-imperialism (after 1850)** – a major cause of **World War I**.

![Fig 1.16 – Workers struggling for their rights](Assets/Chapter_01_Figure_16.png)

### Economic
- Industry and commerce became central; **self-sufficient agrarian economy collapsed** and an **import–export economy** grew; wage-earning working class; **joint-stock/multinational companies**; banking and loans.

### Social
- Positive: new cities, mass-produced goods, jobs, better living for many.
- Negative: overcrowding (e.g., **Vienna's population +20 % in 20 years before 1847, houses +11.5 %**), **class society** (landed aristocrats; middle-class businessmen, bankers, engineers, lawyers vs. working class), 12–14-hour days, child and women labour, slums, drunkenness, **environmental pollution**.
- Labour unions forced governments to pass welfare laws.

![Fig 1.17 – Child labourers in factories during the Industrial Revolution](Assets/Chapter_01_Figure_17.png)

![Fig 1.18 – A factory emitting poisonous gases](Assets/Chapter_01_Figure_18.png)

## 1.3 Effects on Sri Lanka
- No local industrialization in the 19th c., but as a **British colony** it felt the effects: large-scale **tea, coconut, rubber estates** and factories using imported machines.
- **1804** – Governor **Frederick North** started a coffee-pounding mill; machines in tea (drying, grading) and rubber processing; **Macadam road method introduced in 1885**.
- New classes: wage labourers; wealthy educated middle class of estate and factory owners; capitalism; import–export trade; banks.
- **Transport:** **Colombo–Kandy road** (Governor **Edward Barnes**); roads from Kurunegala and Dambulla; stone-and-tar roads; iron bridges replacing wood.
- **Railways:** Governor **Henry Ward**; Kandy line begun **1858**; completed **1863**; Colombo–Kandy train service **August 1867**; extended to Nawalapitiya **1871**, Bandarawela **1899**.
- **Towns:** Gampola, Badulla, Ratnapura, Matale.
- **Communication:** official postal service by **1815** (Colombo, Trincomalee, Galle, Kandy, Jaffna) using coaches – first in Asia; **first stamp 1857**; **340 post offices by 1903**; **telegram service 1858**.

## ⭐ Important Facts
- Revolution began in Britain: textiles, iron/steel, coal.
- Key year list: 1733, 1764, 1769, 1779, 1785 (textile); 1730, 1784, 1856, 1860, 1870 (iron/steel); 1735–36, 1812, 1839 (coal).
- *Laissez-faire* – Adam Smith; neo-imperialism after 1850.

# Chapter 02 – Establishment of British Power in Sri Lanka
*Textbook: Chapter 2 (printed pp. 19–40)*

## 0. Introduction
After the Portuguese and Dutch held the coast, the **British** became the next power. This chapter covers how they established power and the struggles of the first half of the 19th century.

## 2.1 Arrival of the British in Asia
- **East India Company**: **December 1600**, royal charter from **Queen Elizabeth I**. First ships went to **Sumatra**; **William Hawkins** negotiated trade with Mughal emperor **Jahangir**.
- Early focus on South-East Asia (spice islands); rivalry with Portuguese and Dutch. **1623 – Ambon massacre**: the Dutch killed English traders → the English withdrew and concentrated on **India**.
- Trade centres: **Bombay, Calcutta, Madras** (late 17th c.). **French** came from 1664 (Pondicherry, Karikal, Chandernagore). Anglo-French political struggle in India in the 18th century.

## 2.2 British Attention on Sri Lanka
- Ignored Sri Lanka for about a century (weak in India; strong Dutch forts). Interest grew in the first half of the **18th c.** as the **Mughal empire declined**.
- **Two main reasons:** (1) **location** near India; control of the Bay of Bengal; (2) **cinnamon** and other goods (pepper, cardamom, cloves, pearls, gems, ivory).
- **Trincomalee harbour** – faces the Bay of Bengal; protects eastern Indian coast; shelter from the **north-east monsoon**; vital if the French might seize it.

![Map 2.1 – Location of Sri Lanka and Trincomalee harbour](Assets/Chapter_02_Map_01.png)

### Diplomatic contacts with Kandy
| Year | Envoy | King | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1762** | **John Pybus** | Keerthi Sri Rajasinghe | Unsuccessful – English would not help against the Dutch |
| **1782** | **Hugh Boyd** | Rajadhirajasinghe | Unsuccessful |
| **1795** | **Robert Andrews** | Rajadhirajasinghe | King agreed to help; English later refused to share gains after taking Dutch forts |

## 2.3 Establishment of British Power – three stages
1. East India Company captures the coast (**1796**).
2. Coast becomes a **British Crown colony** (**1802**).
3. Kandyan kingdom captured (**1815**).

### Coastal areas
- **French Revolution (1789)** → French invaded Holland (1794); **Stadtholder William V** fled to Britain.
- **Kew Letter** (from Kew Palace): instructed the Dutch to admit British troops; Dutch governor **Van Angelbeek (Funn Englebeck)** received it in Feb 1795 but refused. British attacked **Trincomalee (1795)**; then **Batticaloa, Mullaitivu, Point Pedro, Jaffna, Mannar, Colombo, Galle** surrendered. **Treaty signed 16 February 1796**.

![Fig 2.1 – Dutch Stadtholder William V](Assets/Chapter_02_Figure_01.png)

![Fig 2.2 – Kew Palace, England](Assets/Chapter_02_Figure_02.png)

### East India Company rule (1796–1798) – under Madras
- **Resident**; **Collectors** with **Kachcheri**; **Avumildars**; Madras officials ignorant of local language/culture; Mudaliyars sidelined.
- New taxes in cash – e.g., **coconut-tree tax** (one silver panam = 6 cents per tree for those with more than 10 trees) → **1797 rebellion** across the coast.
- **Meuron Commission** (Brigadier De Meuron) recommended: local officers instead of Madras collectors; abolishing new taxes including coconut tax; separate judicial and revenue officers; restore the **Dutch court system**.

### Dual administration (1798–1802)
- Governor **Frederick North** arrived October **1798**; Company still collected revenue; governor responsible to Crown → conflicts. **Ended 1802**: coast declared **Crown Colony**.

### Conquest of the hill country
- **1798** – Rajadhirajasinghe died; **Pilimathalawe Maha Adikaram** enthroned **Kannasami** as **Sri Wickrama Rajasinghe**; **Muththusami** fled to the British.
- **1800 – MacDowell mission** (with 1164 soldiers and 8 cannons) – king refused entry of the army; mission failed.
- British reasons to take Kandy: threat to coastal security; cost of garrisons; king helped coastal rebels; French might ally with him; need for a **Colombo–Trincomalee land link**.

![Fig 2.3 – Kandy, a naturally secure place](Assets/Chapter_02_Figure_03.png)

![Fig 2.4 – King Sri Wickrama Rajasinghe](Assets/Chapter_02_Figure_04.png)

**1803 invasion** – Troops from Trincomalee and Colombo (Jan–Feb 1803); entered Kandy on **21 February**; installed **Muththusami**; people rallied to the king; **rains, disease, supply cutoff** → British heavily defeated; Muththusami captured.

**1815 invasion**
- Governor **Thomas Maitland** (1805) planned patiently; **John D'Oyly** (Cambridge graduate; studied under monks **Korathota Dhammarama** and **Morathota Dhammakkhandha**; spies disguised as traders and palmists; won Kandyan chiefs).
- Governor **Robert Brownrigg** invaded in **January 1815**.
- **Internal weaknesses:** young king advised by Nayakkar relatives; quarrels with Maha Adikarams (**Pilimathalawe** executed; **Ehelepola**); rift with chiefs and monks; harsh forced labour (Bogambara tank, **Pattirippuwa**); **Sabaragamuwa rebellion**; massacre of Ehelepola's wife and children.
- King abandoned Kandy; captured at **Medamahanuwara**; handed to British on **18 February 1815**.

![Fig 2.5 – John D'Oyly with Kandyan chiefs](Assets/Chapter_02_Figure_05.png)

![Fig 2.6 – Memorial at Medamahanuwara where the king was captured](Assets/Chapter_02_Figure_06.png)

### Kandyan Convention, 2 March 1815
- Signed in **Magul Maduwa** by Brownrigg and Kandyan chiefs (some accounts say signed 10 March).
- Clauses 1–3: king and his family lose their rights; 4: kingdom ceded to British, rights of chiefs protected; 5: **Buddhism, Devales, monks and temples protected**; others – judicial arrangements.
- **Wariyapola Sri Sumangala Thero** is said to have pulled down the English flag and raised the Sinhala flag.
- Ended **more than 2000 years** of unbroken sovereignty; **D'Oyly** became Resident in Kandy.

![Fig 2.7 – Wariyapola Sri Sumangala Thero replacing the English flag with the Sinhala flag](Assets/Chapter_02_Figure_07.png)

## 2.4 Protests against Foreign Rule
### The 1818 struggle (Sept 1817 – Oct 1818)
**Causes**
1. Loss of a king and freedom (distant British king); poem lamenting a kingless land.
2. Religious and cultural problems – distrust of non-Buddhist rulers; traditions of dress/ceremony ignored.
3. Attack on aristocrats' status – cancellation of **Kadawath badda** tax; Muslim **Hajji Marikkar** appointed **Madige Muhandiram** (traditionally Sinhala) → Uva Wellassa chiefs angered.

![Fig 2.8 – Throne of King Sri Wickrama Rajasinghe](Assets/Chapter_02_Figure_08.png)

**Course**
- **Doresami** (claimed throne) in **Wellassa**; **Douglas Wilson** (Badulla agent) sent **Hajji Muhandiram** (killed); Wilson himself killed by an archer.
- **Keppetipola Disawe** was sent but **joined the rebels**; rebels crowned Doresami and made Keppetipola **Maha Adikaram**. **Molligoda** and **Eknaligoda** stayed loyal. **Tooth Relic** fell into rebel hands. **Ehelepola** arrested and taken to Colombo.
- **Martial law (Feb 1818)**; scorched-earth policy; many arrests (Maththamagoda, Kobbekaduwa, Dambawinna, Galagedara, Basnayaka Nilame of Kataragama, Butawe, Ehelepola).
- British said the "king" was not the real Doresami; leaders quarrelled (Keppetipola, Madugalle, Pilimathalawe). **Keppetipola and Madugalle executed on 26 November 1818.**

![Fig 2.9 – Keppetipola Disawe](Assets/Chapter_02_Figure_09.png)

**Failure:** heavy retaliation, lack of weapons/resources, disorganization, exposure of Doresami, Molligoda's help.
**Result:** leaders executed or exiled; property confiscated; loyal chiefs rewarded; **21 November 1818 Proclamation (56 clauses)** curtailed chiefs' power and introduced English law.

### The 1848 struggle (Matale rebellion)
**Causes**
1. **Colebrooke reforms (1833)** – land policy; **Waste Lands (Crown Lands Encroachment) Ordinance of 1840** – land without proof of 30-year ownership confiscated, sold cheaply to Europeans for coffee; abolition of **compulsory service (Rajakariya) and duty labour** weakened **Gam Sabha**; new court system (Cameron) – villagers needed courts, English forms, lawyers and fees; fewer civil servants allowed to plant → officials distant from people.
2. **Torrington's new taxes (1848):** stamp fee; cart and boat (catamaran) tax; shop tax; **gun tax (2½ shillings)**; **"body" (poll) tax** – 3 shillings or 6 days' road labour; **dog tax**; the **Blue Book** survey raised suspicions.

**Course:** Protests in Colombo, Galle, Kandy, Badulla; farmers smashed guns in Badulla; ~**4000 protested at Kandy Kachcheri**; spread to **Matale, Dambulla, Wariyapola, Kurunegala**; leaders **Gongalegoda Banda, Puran Appu, Dingirala, Diyes**; Banda crowned at **Dambulla temple**; Matale the centre. Suppressed within **two months**; Banda whipped and exiled; **Puran Appu executed**; **Rev. Kadahapola Thero shot** after summary trial.
**Failure:** disorganized, untrained rebels, British knowledge of roads, limited area.
**Result:** Governor **Torrington recalled** after a committee; **shop and dog taxes abolished**, others revised; more respect for Buddhism.

## ⭐ Important Facts
- Three stages: 1796 coast → 1802 Crown colony → 1815 Kandy.
- Kandyan Convention 2 March 1815; rebellions 1818 and 1848.
- Leaders: Keppetipola (1818), Gongalegoda Banda and Puran Appu (1848).

# Chapter 03 – National Renaissance in Sri Lanka
*Textbook: Chapter 3 (printed pp. 41–50)*

## 0. Introduction
- After the 1848 struggle the independence movement had two phases: **1850–1915 national and religious renaissance**; **1915–1948 political agitation**.
- After 1815, traditional religions and culture faced obstacles → Buddhists, Hindus and Muslims awakened in the late 19th century.

## 3.1 Background of the National Renaissance
- While the Kandyan kingdom was independent, Portuguese and Dutch could spread religion only on the coast; after 1815 Western culture spread island-wide.

### Missionary organizations
| Organization | Year arrived | Area |
|---|---|---|
| **Baptist** | 1812 | Colombo |
| **Wesleyan (Methodist)** | 1814 | North and East |
| **London Missionary Society** | 1804 (listed first) | – |
| **American Mission** | 1816 | Jaffna (North) |
| **Church Missionary Society (CMS)** | 1818 | Colombo, Galle, Kalpitiya, Mannar, Kandy |
- Methods: **writing, preaching and education**.
- **Printing:** learned Sinhala and Tamil; Bible translations; publications – **Masika Thegga (1832, Church Missionary Society, Kotte/Uragasmanhandiya)**, **Lanka Nidhanaya (1840, Religious Tract Society)**, and an 1842 Baptist publication (as in the text table).
- **Education:** mission schools with government assistance; English was needed for government posts, so Sinhala and Tamil children attended them.

## 3.2 Buddhist Renaissance
Needs: answer missionary criticism of image worship; improve native-language education (no government support).

### Pirivenas
| Pirivena | Founder | Year / place |
|---|---|---|
| **Parama Dhamma Chethiya Pirivena** | **Walane Siddhartha Thero** | Ratmalana |
| **Vidyodaya Pirivena** | **Hikkaduwe Sri Sumangala Thero** | **1873**, Maligakanda |
| **Vidyalankara Pirivena** | **Ratmalane Dhammaloka Thero** | **1875**, Peliyagoda |

### Printing presses
- **Bulathgama Dhammalankara Siri Sumanathissa Thero** got money from the **King of Siam (Thailand)** and set up **Lankopakara Press, Galle, 1862**.
- Others: **Lankabhinava Visruta, Kavyakathika, Sudarshana, Sarasavi Sandaresa** presses.
- Publications: **Lakmini Pahana, Lankaloka, Sarasavi Sandaresa, Sinhala Jathiya, Sinhala Bauddhaya**.

### Public debates – "Pancha Maha Vadaya" (Five Great Debates)
| Debate | Year |
|---|---|
| Baddegama | 1865 |
| Waragoda | 1865 |
| Udanwita | 1866 |
| Gampola | 1871 |
| **Panadura** | **1873** – most famous |
- Leader **Migettuwatte Gunananda Thero** (Kotahena Deepaduththaramaya), supported by **Hikkaduwe Sumangala, Waskaduwe Subuthi, Pothuwila Indrajothi, Ratmalane Dhammaloka**.
- **Henry Steel Olcott** came after reading an account of the **Panadura debate**.

### Buddhist schools
- Olcott became Buddhist; helped start the **Colombo Parama Vignanartha Movement (June 1880)**; also the **Buddhist Theosophical Society** fund; fundraising tours with monks.
- Supporters: Olcott, Hikkaduwe Sumangala, Dhammaloka, Subuthi, Gunananda, **Don Carolis Hewavitarana**, **Amaris de Silva Batuwanthudawe**, **D.S. Senanayake**, **Anagarika Dharmapala**, **Jeremias Dias**.
- Schools: **Ananda Vidyalaya (Colombo)**, **Dharmaraja (Kandy)**, **Mahinda (Galle)**, **Vijaya (Matale)**, **Jinaraja (Gampola)**, **Anuruddha (Nawalapitiya)**.
- Other achievements: **Vesak poya declared a public holiday**; **Buddhist flag** introduced.

### Movements
**1. Patriotism and nationalism** – **Anagarika Dharmapala** (public preaching; *Sinhala Bauddhaya*, *Mahabodhi*), **Piyadasa Sirisena** (novels, *Sinhala Jathiya*), **Walisinghe Harischandra**, **John de Silva** (drama).

![Fig 3.5 – Anagarika Dharmapala](Assets/Chapter_03_Figure_05.png)

**2. Protection of Buddhist sites** – Anuradhapura and Polonnaruwa ruins; scholars **George Turner, T.W. Rhys Davids, H.C.P. Bell, Henry Parker**; Harischandra's movement at Anuradhapura; Dharmapala at **Buddha Gaya**; **Ruwanweliseya restoration c. 1870**.

**3. Temperance movement** – Alcoholism had become a social problem; Dharmapala (preaching and papers), Piyadasa Sirisena (novel dialogues), **John de Silva** (drama *Sinhala Parabhawa*, "Sura Act").

## 3.3 Hindu Religious Renaissance
- Leader **Arumuga Navalar** (educated in a mission school; served a Methodist priest).
- Press at **Vannarpannai (Jaffna)**; newspaper **Udayabhanu**; Tamil textbooks (3 volumes); translated *Periya Puranam* and *Skanda Puranam*.
- **Vannarpannai Saiva Prakasa Vidyalaya (1849)**; later Saiva Paripalana Sabai and Hindu Education Society; **Sivangala school (1872)** → **Jaffna Hindu College**.
- Successors: **Sir Ponnambalam Ramanathan** (defended Buddhists during **1915** martial law) and **Ponnambalam Arunachalam** (founded **Ceylon National Congress, 1919**).

- **T.B. Jayah** – principal of Zahira College, politician, founded schools for Muslims.

# Chapter 04 – Political Changes in Sri Lanka under the British
*Textbook: Chapter 4 (printed pp. 51–64)*

## 0. Introduction
British rule over the whole island lasted **133 years (1815–1948)**. The British introduced constitutional reforms from time to time; Sri Lankans pressed for more power until independence in 1948.

## 4.1 Colebrooke–Cameron Reforms (1833)
- After 1815 expenditure exceeded revenue; London did not want to subsidise colonies.
- **W.M.G. Colebrooke** (arrived **1829**) investigated administration and finance; **Charles Hay Cameron** (arrived **1830**) investigated the judicial system. Reforms introduced **1833**.

### Features
- **Executive Council** and **Legislative Council** established to limit the governor's power.
- Unified administration of **Kandyan and low-country** areas.
- **Five provinces** (see Map 4.1).
- **Rajakariya (compulsory service) abolished.**
- **End of government trade monopolies.**

### Legislative Council (1833) – 15 members
| Type | Number |
|---|---|
| Official | 9 |
| Unofficial | 6 – Europeans 3; Sinhalese 1; Tamil 1; Burgher 1 |
- Governor nominated the 6 unofficials – **communal (nationalist) representation** – harmful later; council loyal to governor; officials in majority.
- **Executive Council** – colonial secretary, treasurer, auditor-general, surveyor-general, collector of customs; governor must consult it on finance but could overrule (reporting to Colonial Secretary).

![Map 4.1 – Five provinces under the Colebrooke reforms](Assets/Chapter_04_Map_01.png)

### European struggle
- European planters wanted infrastructure (roads, railways) and a larger elected unofficial share; methods: questions in council, petitions, newspaper articles, associations – **Lanka Congress (1865, George Wall)**.
- Result: unofficial members raised to **8** (two added: Kandyan Sinhalese and Muslims). Sri Lankans learned from this.

## 4.2 Crewe–McCallum Reforms (1910)
### Background – demands of the Sri Lankan middle class
- New Sri Lankan middle class by early 20th century; no political change for ~70 years.
- **James Pieris** petitioned the Colonial Secretary (Dec **1908**); clubs followed (**Low Country Products Association, Chilaw Club, Jaffna Club**).
- Demands: more unofficials; end communal representation; territorial (provincial) elections; more power for the council. Governor **Henry McCallum** opposed, but Colonial Secretary **Lord Crewe** announced reforms (Nov 1910); operated from **1912**.

![Fig 4.1 – James Pieris](Assets/Chapter_04_Figure_01.png)

### 1910 Legislative Council – 21 members
| Type | Number |
|---|---|
| Official | 11 |
| Unofficial | 10 – nominated 6 (communal); elected 4 (2 Europeans, 1 Burgher, **1 educated Ceylonese**) |
- **First election** to the Council (limited franchise); **Ponnambalam Ramanathan** elected as educated Ceylonese representative (1912); officials still a majority.

### Temperance movement 1912–1915
- Opposed new **Excise Ordinance (1912)** allowing new toddy taverns; middle class and renaissance activists united; network of temperance societies under a central council in Colombo; criticised the government.
- **1915 Sinhalese–Muslim riots** (Kandy): government delayed action, imposed **martial law**, arrested temperance leaders and banned newspapers. Leaders who emerged: **F.R. Senanayake, D.S. Senanayake, D.B. Jayatilaka**.

### Indian influence
- **Indian National Congress (1885)**; leaders Surendranath Banerjee, Tilak, Gandhi, Nehru – **Swaraj**. **Gandhi visited Sri Lanka in 1927**. Ceylon leaders realised petitions were not enough.

![Fig 4.2 – Mahatma Gandhi](Assets/Chapter_04_Figure_02.png)

### Ceylon National Congress (December 1919)
- Formed after the 1915 repression to unite small groups; **first president: Ponnambalam Arunachalam**; united Sinhalese, Tamils and Muslims.

![Fig 4.3 – D.B. Jayatilaka](Assets/Chapter_04_Figure_03.png)

| Type | Number |
|---|---|
| Legislative Council | 37 |
| Official | 14 |
| Unofficial | 23 – nominated 7; elected 16 (territorial) |
- First **unofficial majority**; **territorial representation** introduced; communal representation kept; governor's nominated 7 + 14 officials could outvote. Congress dissatisfied.

| Type | Number |
|---|---|
| Legislative Council | 49 |
| Official | 12 |

## 4.3 Donoughmore Constitution (1931)
- Commission headed by **Lord Donoughmore**, visited **1927**; constitution came into force **1931**.
### State Council – 61 members
| Group | Number |
|---|---|
| Elected (territorial) | 50 |
| Nominated by governor | 8 |
| **Officers of State** (Chief Secretary, Financial Secretary, Legal Secretary) | 3 |
- Speaker elected by council.

### Universal franchise
- Before 1931 voters were only about **4 %** (1924) and women excluded. The Commission recommended **all men and women over 21** → **universal franchise 1931** (first in Asia).

### Executive committee system
- State Council divided into **7 committees**; each chairman became a **minister**: Home Affairs, Agriculture & Lands, Local Administration, Health, Education, Communications & Works, Labour, Industry & Commerce → 7 ministers. Projects: **agricultural colonies**, **free education**.
- Governor's power reduced, but **defence, finance, legal affairs** stayed with the three Officers of State.

## 4.4 Protests for Independence
- From 1931: demands to curtail governor's powers and hand over officers' portfolios.
### Suriyamal (Sunflower) Movement (1933)
- **Lanka Sama Samaja Party** leaders **N.M. Perera and Colvin R. de Silva**; countered **Poppy Day** (11 November, remembrance of WWI soldiers; funds sent to Britain). Sunflower sales for ex-servicemen and the poor – poppy = imperial loyalty/slavery; sunflower = independence.

- **Soulbury Commission (1944)**; report **1945** recommended a parliamentary system; **State Council approved in November 1945** (see Chapter 6).

# Chapter 05 – Social Changes in Sri Lanka under the British
*Textbook: Chapter 5 (printed pp. 65–78)*

## 0. Introduction
By independence (1948) the traditional political, socio-economic and cultural systems had changed drastically. This chapter covers economic, social and cultural changes under British rule.

## 5.1 Economic Changes
### Before the British
- Traditional socio-economic system rooted in the Anuradhapura era and Buddhism. **Portuguese** (16th c.) neglected agriculture for trade; **Dutch** grew cinnamon and pepper for export, started minor industries and daily wages.
- Little change because the **Kandyan kingdom** held most of the island.
- **Village-centred, self-sufficient economy**; mutual help (**Aththama**); little money; no wage labour.
- **Gamsabha** (village council): settled minor disputes; organised community works (tanks, bunds, roads, pagodas) through traditional labour; a minor court by Kandyan times.
- **Rajakariya (compulsory service)** – services to the king without pay for a set number of days per year; **service tenure lands** (*nindagam/service lands*) and **duty based on land occupation**.

### Economic reforms of the Colebrooke Commission (1833)
| Reform | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Sale of land** to locals and foreigners; civil servants could plant | Allow private enterprise; end traditional land tenure obstacle |
| **Abolition of rajakariya** | Labour could be bought – wage-labour class began |
| **End of cinnamon and salt monopolies** | Encourage private trade |
| **Fish and land taxes** – replaced direct "farming out" by permits for boats; land tax in cash | Simplify revenue |
| **Savings bank** | Cheap credit for planters |

### Development of plantations
Few locals had capital, so **foreign investors** bought thousands of acres.

| Crop | Key facts |
|---|---|
| **Coffee** (see Fig. below) | Small scale under Dutch; boom after 1833; **Edward Barnes (1824–31)** cut export tax and freed coffee workers from compulsory service; cheap **Indian labour**; **1837–1847** rapid expansion in Dumbara, Gampola, Peradeniya, Matale, Badulla; exports in 1844 five times 1834; **1844** free-trade policy hit prices; recovered after 1850; destroyed by **leaf disease *Hemileia vastatrix* from 1869** |
| **Cinchona and cocoa** | Cinchona (source of quinine; limited demand; only high altitudes); cocoa (needed shade; ≤1200 acres; Matale) – failed to replace coffee |
| **Tea** | 1867 planters sent workers to **Assam**; **James Taylor** experimented at **Loolecondera estate**; 500 acres by **1875**; **400 000 acres by 1894**; Central, Uva, Sabaragamuwa then South and West; factories and machines; South-Indian labour; major export. Prices fell after WWI and 1929 Depression but recovered |
| **Coconut** | From 1850 interest grew after coffee fell; **65 % of owners were Sri Lankans by 1880**; Kurunegala, Chilaw, Gampaha; **27 % of export earnings by 1920**; **Coconut Research Institute at Lunuwila**; half the crop used locally |
| **Rubber** | Introduced c. **1877**; boomed after 1900 (motor vehicles, suitable varieties, tea price fall); began in Kalutara, then Western, Sabaragamuwa, Southern, Central; **30 % of export earnings by 1920**; **Rubber Research Institute at Agalawatta** |

## 5.2 Social Change
### Emergence of a middle class

- Old **aristocrats/elite** (Kandyan chiefs etc.) declined; new local wealth from **contracting, arrack renting, graphite mining, trade, transport, coconut plantations**; English education opened careers (lawyers, engineers, surveyors, clerks, civil servants) → **educated middle class** (city-dwellers following Western culture) which led 20th-century politics.

### Labour class
- Plantations needed labour → **Indian (South Indian) Tamil labourers** brought in thousands for low wages. Urban labourers (Colombo, Galle, Kandy) – dock workers, launderers, carters, road and rail workers – long hours, no insurance.
- **Trade unions and strikes:** printers, Colombo launderers, carters' strikes; **A.E. Goonesinha** (leader 1922–1935; **Ceylon Labour Union / Ceylon Workers' Congress**); **LSSP** unions; **Natesa Aiyar** organised estate workers.

### Cultural change
- Western education and English spread after Colebrooke; mission and government-aided schools run by foreigners. Europeans' clubs and holiday resorts. Western dress, customs, greetings, architecture, food entered Sri Lankan life.

## 5.3 Advantages of British rule
Despite 133 years of exploitation: English language; **transport and communication**; **export plantations**; benefits of the industrial revolution; **parliamentary system**.

## 5.4 Disadvantages
Majority poor at independence; unequal income; decline of indigenous culture; alcohol spread; **landless class**; collapse of self-sufficient economy and indigenous knowledge; destruction of **Gamsabha**.

# Chapter 06 – Receiving of Independence to Sri Lanka
*Textbook: Chapter 6 (printed pp. 79–105)*

## 0. Introduction
Independence in **1948** gave Sri Lanka a parliamentary system of representative democracy; new constitutions followed in **1972** and **1978**. This chapter also covers development after independence.

## 6.1 Soulbury Reforms and Independence
- **Soulbury Commission** arrived **December 1944**; report **September 1945** – full internal self-government; State Council passed the white paper; **Royal Order in Council May 1946**.
- Britain was silent on independence before the **1947** election; **D.S. Senanayake** presented the case; Colonial Secretary announced independence **within the Commonwealth** two months before the election.
- **United National Party** formed (1946) by D.S. Senanayake.

### 1947 Parliamentary election
| Party | Seats |
|---|---|
| **United National Party** | **42** |
| Lanka Sama Samaja Party | 10 |
| Tamil Congress | 7 |
| Lanka Indian Congress | 6 |
| Bolshevik Leninist Party | 5 |
| Lanka Communist Party | 3 |
| Labour Party | 1 |
| Independents | 21 |
| **Total** | **95** |
- D.S. Senanayake formed the government with independents' support.
- **November 1947 agreements:** **Defence agreement** (British use of bases) and **External Affairs agreement** (British help with foreign relations).
- **Independence Act**; **Order in Council 19 December 1947**; **independence on 4 February 1948**; Parliament opened **10 February 1948** (Senanayake raised the Sri Lankan flag).

### Soulbury Constitution (1947–1972)
| Institution | Details |
|---|---|
| **Governor-General** | Nominal head; represented the Queen; acted on PM's advice |
| **Parliament** | **House of Representatives 101** (95 elected + 6 nominated by Governor-General for minorities); term **5 years**; **Senate 30** (15 elected by House, 15 nominated) |
| **Prime Minister and Cabinet** | PM commands majority of the House; cabinet holds executive power; **Westminster system – collective responsibility**; PM held defence and external affairs; Justice minister and one other from Senate |
| **Judiciary** | Supreme Court; Governor-General appoints Chief Justice and judges; **Judicial Service Commission** |

### Political party system
- **Two-party** (Britain, USA) vs **multi-party** (France, Germany, India, Sri Lanka). Party politics began in the **1947** election.
- **LSSP (1935)** – N.M. Perera, Colvin R. de Silva, Philip Gunawardena; goal: socialist society; **Communist Party (1943)** – split under **S.A. Wickramasinghe**.
- **UNP (1946)** – D.S. Senanayake; included most of the Ceylon National Congress and Sinhala Maha Sabha; won 54 seats in 1952 and formed government.
- **SLFP (1951)** – **S.W.R.D. Bandaranaike** left UNP; won 9 seats (the textbook dates this election to 1951); contested 1956 in alliance as the **Mahajana Eksath Peramuna (MEP)** coalition – won **51 seats in 1956**; he was PM 1956–59; after his assassination **Sirimavo Bandaranaike** became leader.
- Other parties: **Labour Party (A.E. Goonesinha)**, **Tamil Congress (G.G. Ponnambalam)** – 7 seats 1947; **Federal Party (1949, S.J.V. Chelvanayakam)** which weakened Tamil Congress.
- Many small parties → coalition governments.

![Fig 6.1 – S.W.R.D. Bandaranaike's cabinet](Assets/Chapter_06_Figure_01.png)

### 1956 election and "social revolution"
- D.S. Senanayake died March **1952**; **Dudley Senanayake** PM; resigned 1953 over rice price protests; **John Kotelawala** PM; dissolved Parliament in **February 1956**.
- MEP = SLFP + Philip Gunawardena's **Revolutionary Socialist Party** + Vijayananda Dahanayake's **Language Congress (Bhasha Peramuna)** + independents (e.g., I.M.R.A. Iriyagolla); no-contest pact with **LSSP**.
- Issue: **Sinhala as official language**. Support of the **Pancha Maha Balavegaya** (monks, native physicians, teachers, farmers, workers).
- Seats: **MEP 51**, LSSP 14, Federal Party 10, UNP **8**.
- **Bandaranaike's policies:** Sinhala as state language; ended 1947 defence and external-affairs agreements; **non-aligned foreign policy**; nationalised harbours and **buses**; promoted local industry and agriculture; **Vidyodaya and Vidyalankara pirivenas made universities**.

### Republic of 1972
- Soulbury independence incomplete (Queen still head; restrictions on Parliament).
- **1970** election: **United Front (SLFP + LSSP + Communist Party)** won; **Sirimavo Bandaranaike** PM; Constituent Assembly → **Constitution of 22 May 1972**.
- **Features:** name **Ceylon → Sri Lanka**; ended constitutional link with Britain; **unitary republic**; **fundamental rights** (Article 18: impartial law; no deprivation of life/liberty except by law; freedom of thought, conscience, religion; peaceful assembly and expression).
- **President** – nominal executive, appointed by PM; **William Gopallawa** first president.
- **National State Assembly** – sole legislature; term **6 years**.
- **PM and Cabinet** – executive.
- **Courts:** Supreme Court, High Court, District Court, Magistrate's Court, **Constitutional Court**; Judicial Service Advisory and Disciplinary Boards.

![Fig 6.2 – William Gopallawa, first President](Assets/Chapter_06_Figure_02.png)

### 1978 Constitution
- **1977 election:** UNP won **140 of 168** seats (> two-thirds); Tamil United Liberation Front **18**; SLFP **8**; plus Ceylon Workers' Congress 1 and one independent. **J.R. Jayewardene** introduced an **executive presidency** (from 1978).
- Name: **Democratic Socialist Republic of Sri Lanka**; unitary state; sovereignty of the people (exercised by Parliament, the President, the judiciary and by referendum).

![Fig 6.3 – J.R. Jayewardene](Assets/Chapter_06_Figure_03.png)

| Feature | 1978 Constitution |
|---|---|
| **President** | Elected by the people (term **6 years**); head of state, government, executive, Parliament and commander-in-chief (Article 30). Powers: summon/prorogue/dissolve Parliament; present policy; appoint PM and ministers; appoint Supreme Court/Appeal Court judges; **pardon**; declare war and peace; immune from suit for official acts |
| **PM and Cabinet** | Cabinet appointed from MPs; collectively responsible to Parliament |
| **Parliament** | **225 members** – **196 elected** (proportional representation) + **29 national list** |
| **Judiciary** | Supreme, Appeal, High, District, Family, Magistrate's and Primary Courts. **Supreme Court** – fundamental rights, impeachment, privileges, election petitions; **Judicial Service Commission** (Chief Justice + two judges) |
| **Fundamental rights** | Chapter III – wider and more detailed than 1972: equality before law, freedom of speech and opinion, freedom from harassment, religion, occupation; a person may petition the **Supreme Court within three months** (Article 129, as stated in the textbook); complaint to the **Parliamentary Commissioner (Ombudsman)** for public-sector rights |
| **Elections** | **Presidential** – whole island one electorate; 50 % needed; **J.R. Jayewardene** (first, 1978; re-elected 1982); later **R. Premadasa, D.B. Wijetunga (not elected), Chandrika Kumaratunga, Mahinda Rajapaksa, Maithripala Sirisena** |
| **Proportional representation** | Vote for party then preference votes |
| **Referendum** | First introduced in 1978; **only one held: 1982** to extend Parliament; needs > 50 % |

## 6.2 Development after Independence
### Agriculture
- British neglected local food production; State Council (1931–47) under **D.S. Senanayake** (Minister of Agriculture) started **dry-zone farming settlements (colonization)**.
- **Aims:** relieve wet-zone population and unemployment; increase food production; restore old tanks; settled farming community.
- Dry zone abandoned after **Magha (700 years earlier)** was re-used.
- **Challenges:** selecting settlers, clearing forest, irrigation, financial help until first harvest, health and education, marketing.
- Pre-independence colonies: **Kagama, Minneriya, Nachchaduwa, Beragama, Minipe, Parakrama Samudra, Ridi Bendi Ela, Sangili Kanadarawa, Onichchei**. After independence (to 1953): 15 more – **Bathmadika, Devahuwa, Giritale, Paniyankadawala, Iranamadu, Gal Oya, Kantale, Sorabora Wewa** etc. **Gal Oya** – first **multi-purpose** project.

- Other paddy measures: **guaranteed price (1948)**; paddy research (e.g., **Bathalegoda breeding centre**) and new varieties; **Govijana Sewa (farming service) centres**; loans through state banks.

### Commercial crops
- **Land Reform Law 1972:** ceilings – **10 ha (25 acres)** paddy, **20 ha (50 acres)** other.
- **1975 land reform:** companies' estates nationalised – about **62 %** to state; **10 %** of state land given to small farmers.
- Institutions: **State Plantations Corporation (1958)**, **Up-country Estate Development Bureau (1976)** and **People's Estate Development Bureau (1976)**.
- **1952 bilateral agreement with China** gave a stable rubber market; **re-planting aid scheme 1977** (plants and fertilizer for rubber, later tea).
- Small holders (1–10 acres) in West, South and Sabaragamuwa.
- **Minor export crops** (coffee, cocoa, cardamom, cloves, nutmeg, pepper, cinnamon): Tea Commission report 1968; Minor Export Crops research station **Matale 1972**; later Kundasale, Delpitiya, Narammala.

### Industrialisation
- None under British; WWII stimulated local industries (acetic acid, porcelain, paper, soap, perfumes). **1934 Bank Commission** recommended local industry. **1952 World Bank mission**; **1955 White Paper**; **Ekala (1959)** and **Ratmalana (1960)** industrial zones.
- After 1960: **People's Bank (1961)**; **Standards Bureau (1966)**; **import substitution 1970–77**; large industries (cement, steel, sugar, plywood).
- After **1977**: export-oriented industries (e.g., garments) – the **Greater Colombo Economic Commission (1978; "Mega Colombo" in the text)**, the **Katunayake export processing zone (1978)**, later Biyagama and Koggala.

### Education
- **Free education** (C.W.W. Kannangara, Minister of Education, State Council): grade 1 to university; continued thereafter.
- Milestones: more schools; curriculum and teacher-training; free textbooks, meals, medical care, uniforms and concessionary transport; **Ceylon University 1942 (Colombo)** → **Peradeniya 1952**; Vidyodaya → **Sri Jayewardenepura**, Vidyalankara → **Kelaniya**; **Moratuwa/Katubedda 1972**, **Jaffna 1974**.

- **Rice subsidy** – **ration book (Hal Potha)**; **food stamps from 1977**.
- Housing: flats and housing schemes in cities; rural aid.

# Chapter 07 – Significant Revolutions in the World
*Textbook: Chapter 7 (printed pp. 106–125)*

## 0. Introduction
A **revolution** is a significant change in a field within a short time (industrial revolution, religious renaissance, and the political revolutions: **American, French, Russian**).

## 7.1 American Independence Struggle
### Background
- Columbus landed in the Bahamas (1492); **Amerigo Vespucci** found the continent; a German geographer named it **America**. Columbus thought he had reached India, so natives were called "Indians"; northern natives = **Red Indians**.
- Colonised by Portugal, Spain, Holland, France, Britain. Reasons: **religious freedom** (Catholic–Protestant conflicts), prosperity through land and trade, encouragement of rulers.
- Portuguese and Spanish settled the south; British and French the north.

### The Thirteen Colonies
Virginia (1607 – first), Massachusetts, New York, New Hampshire, Maryland, Connecticut, Rhode Island, Delaware, North Carolina, New Jersey, South Carolina, Pennsylvania, **Georgia (1733 – last)**. Twelve were founded between 1607 and 1682.
- Founded by individuals, groups and companies with a British royal charter; each had a **governor** and an elected **legislative council**; wide **self-government** because of distance and Britain's wars.

![Fig 7.1 – The thirteen states established by the British in America](Assets/Chapter_07_Figure_01.png)

### Why settlers needed Britain before 1763
1. **French threat** from the north – **Seven Years' War (1756–1763)** ended French power.
2. **Red Indians** – attacks reduced after France's defeat.

### Causes of the revolution
- British mercantile policy: colonies could not make goods made in Britain; certain products (tobacco, sugar, cotton, beeswax, maize, copper) sold only to Britain; trade only via Britain.
- After 1763 Britain, burdened with war debts, asked colonies to share the cost. **George Grenville** tightened tax collection, naval patrols against smuggling. Acts (1764–65): **Sugar Act, Currency Act, Stamp Act**.
- **Stamp Act (1765)** – tax on all printed documents; hit traders, lawyers, printers; boycotts.
- Further duties 1767 (glass, lead, paper, tea); **Quartering Act** (housing and funding troops); dissolving colonial assemblies.
- **Slogan: "No taxation without representation"** (text: "no member, no tax").

### Events
- Stamp Act repealed, but Britain **declared its right to legislate for the colonies**.
- **1767 Townshend duties**; **Boston Massacre (5 killed)**; all duties repealed except tea (**1770**).
- **1773 Tea Act** – monopoly to one company; **December 1773 Boston Tea Party** – 342 chests of tea thrown into the sea; considered the start of the revolution.
- Britain closed **Boston harbour** and reformed Massachusetts' government.
- **First Continental Congress (Philadelphia, 1774)** – 12 colonies; denied Parliament's authority; boycott of British goods; arming citizens.
- **1775 Lexington** – first armed clashes; Congress formed a **Continental Army** under **George Washington**.
- **Declaration of Independence – 4 July 1776** (rights are inborn and governments exist to protect them).
- War: British won early; later failed (distance, unfamiliar land); **France**, then Spain and Holland aided America.
- **Treaty of Paris, 1783** recognised independence; **Constitution 1789**; **George Washington first president**.

![Fig 7.2 – The Boston Tea Party](Assets/Chapter_07_Figure_02.png)

### Results
- Thirteen states united and won independence – example to other colonies; birth of the **USA**; enthusiasm for human rights; first **written unified constitution**.

## 7.2 French Revolution (1789)
France was prosperous in the 18th c. but suffered from weak, corrupt rule under **Louis XVI**.

### Political causes
- **Bourbon absolute monarchy**; divine right; **Louis XIV: "I am the state"**, lettres de cachet (arrest without trial); **Bastille** prison = symbol.
- **Versailles palace** – extravagance; king isolated; only aristocrats and priests had access; **Queen Marie Antoinette** meddled.
- Inefficient administration; **intendants** vs. nobles; **Estates-General not summoned for 175 years**.

### Social causes – three estates
| Estate | Group | Privileges |
|---|---|---|
| First | Clergy | Tax-exempt; wealthy |
| Second | Aristocrats | Owned most land; hunting/fishing rights; feudal dues; mills/ovens monopolies; tax-exempt |
| Third | Commoners – **middle class** (educated, rich, no political recognition), **peasants (92 % of population)** and **labourers** | Paid all taxes |
- Idea emerged that status should depend on ability and wealth, not birth.

### Economic causes
- **Bankrupt treasury** – royal extravagance, wars (Seven Years' War, American war support), inefficient taxation.
- Taxes ~60 % of income: **Taille** (land and houses), **Gabelle** (salt; 7 lb per adult), **Capitation** (poll tax, 1695).
- Internal customs barriers (a wine vessel from the south to Paris paid tax at 40 places, 2 weeks extra).

### Philosophers
| Thinker | Work | Idea |
|---|---|---|
| **Montesquieu** | *The Spirit of the Laws* | Separation of powers: legislature, executive, judiciary |
| **Voltaire** | – | Freedom of expression; criticised monarchy and Church monopoly |
| **Rousseau** | *The Social Contract* | Popular sovereignty; people may remove bad rulers |
- Returning soldiers from America spread ideas of liberty.

### The Revolution
- 1789: king summoned the **Estates-General**; nobles insisted on voting by estate; **Third Estate** formed the **National Assembly** and took the **Tennis Court Oath** (not to disband until a constitution was written).
- **14 July 1789 – storming of the Bastille** – start of the revolution; spread across France; **Lafayette** led the National Guard; **Louis XVI and Marie Antoinette were tried and executed**.

![Fig 7.12 – Storming of the Bastille](Assets/Chapter_07_Figure_12.png)

### Results
- **Declaration of the Rights of Man and the Citizen** (text: 1791 convention): rights are inborn; equality; right to take part in law-making; freedom of speech and religion; slogan **Liberty, Equality, Fraternity**.
- End of monarchy; relief for peasants and workers; **political instability 1789–1799** → **Napoleon Bonaparte** seized power in **1799**.

## 7.3 Russian Revolution (1917)
### Causes
- Huge empire (1/6 of world's land); **Tsar's autocracy**, army, police, Church; feudal society; peasants like slaves; small working class from industry; weak middle class.
- **Defeat by Japan (1904–05)** → **1905 revolution** (peasants, strikes, navy mutiny – failed); Tsar's limited reforms (Duma) unsatisfactory; **Bolsheviks under Lenin** worked secretly.
- **World War I (1914)** – heavy losses, forced conscription, factories turned to arms, food shortages, bread prices soared. Slogan: **Peace, Land, Bread**.

### March 1917 revolution
- Began with a strike in a textile factory → general strike in three days; soldiers joined; **Tsar abdicated**; **provisional government under Kerensky** continued the war and sided with capitalists – failed peasants and soldiers.

### October (November) 1917 revolution
- **Bolsheviks (majority, communists; Lenin and Trotsky)** vs **Mensheviks (minority, reformists)**; **Soviets**; Lenin: *"If we do not seize power now, history will not forgive us."*
- Armed groups seized railways, banks, post offices; little resistance. Russian calendar = October; world calendar = November.

![Fig 7.13 – V.I. Lenin](Assets/Chapter_07_Figure_13.png)

![Fig 7.14 – Leon Trotsky](Assets/Chapter_07_Figure_14.png)

### Results
- Bolshevik (later **Communist**) government under Lenin; **peace with Germany**; private land abolished and distributed, later **collective farms**; factories, banks and transport **nationalised** under soviets; Tsarist officials replaced; state became **Union of Soviet Socialist Republics** (text: "United States of Soviet Russia").
- **Socialism** – equal rights to society's wealth, equal opportunity, equitable distribution; common over private interest; end of class distinctions.
- After WWII: two camps – **capitalist (USA)** and **socialist (USSR)** blocs; others **non-aligned** (Sri Lanka). Socialist elements in constitutions of China, East Germany, South Korea [as stated], Cuba.

- American: 1607 Virginia; 1765 Stamp Act; 1773 Tea Party; 4 July 1776; 1783 Paris; 1789 constitution.
- French: 14 July 1789; Tennis Court Oath; Napoleon 1799.
- Russian: March and October 1917; Lenin; Bolsheviks.

# Chapter 08 – World Wars and Conventions
*Textbook: Chapter 8 (printed pp. 126–154)*

## 0. Introduction
Two world wars occurred in the first half of the 20th century; both began in Europe and spread to other continents because powerful states took part. Afterwards leaders created conventions and organisations to prevent war.

## 8.1 World War I (1914–1918)
![Map 8.1 – How World War I spread over the world](Assets/Chapter_08_Map_01.png)

### Reasons
| # | Reason | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Franco-German rivalry** | ~350 German-speaking states unified by **Otto von Bismarck** (1870); war with France; Germany took **Alsace and Lorraine** → permanent enmity |
| 2 | **Kaiser William II (from 1887)** | Wanted a colonial empire against Bismarck's aims; Germany armed; Britain built up forces |
| 3 | **Colonial competition** | Britain largest empire; also Belgium, France, Portugal, Spain, Holland; industrial Germany needed markets and was viewed as a challenger |
| 4 | **Arms race and nationalism** | Germany, Britain, France, Russia; German and Italian unification fostered aggressive nationalism |
| 5 | **Bismarck's isolation of France** → **two camps** | **Central Powers** – Germany, Austria (Austria–Hungary), Italy; **Allies** – Britain, France, Russia |
| 6 | **Sarajevo incident (28 June 1914)** | Austrian heir **Archduke Franz Ferdinand and his wife** assassinated in **Sarajevo (Bosnia)** by Serbians; Austria declared war on Serbia on **28 July 1914** |

### Spread of the war
- Serbia asked **Russia** for help; **Germany** declared war on Russia (**1 August 1914**) and France; invaded **Belgium**; **Britain** declared war on Germany; colonies were drawn in. German submarines sank one in four British ships.
- Camps in the text: **Allies** – France, Britain, Russia, USA; **Central Powers** – Germany, Austria, Italy, Turkey, Bulgaria, Hungary (note: Italy later secretly joined the Allies and declared war on Germany).
- **USA** joined after the **Lusitania** was sunk by a German submarine; American food, 2 million soldiers and modern weapons helped defeat Germany. German navy mutiny; emperor abdicated; **war ended 1918**.

![Fig 8.5 – The ship Lusitania](Assets/Chapter_08_Figure_05.png)

### Results
- Victory of the Allies; heavy loss of life; **Paris Peace Conference (January 1919)** – **Woodrow Wilson (USA), Lloyd George (Britain), Clemenceau (France), Orlando (Italy)**.
- **Treaty of Versailles** with Germany: heavy reparations; limits on army and arms; **Alsace-Lorraine** returned to France; colonies divided; empires (**German, Austro-Hungarian, Ottoman**) collapsed; new states (**Finland, Poland, Czechoslovakia**); economic collapse in Europe; USA and Britain became powerful.

## 8.2 League of Nations
- Proposed by **Woodrow Wilson** at the 1919 Paris talks; established **10 January 1920** with **42 members**.
- **Aims:** settle disputes peacefully; prevent future wars; promote understanding; protect small states; world peace.
- **Successes (~20 years):** prevented wars (Sweden–Finland, Germany–Poland, Greece–Bulgaria, Iran–Turkey); refugee rehabilitation; supervised mandates; reduced slavery and drug trade.
- **Failures:** USA never joined (Senate refused); Russia excluded; no military force or enforcement power; disunity among powers; ignored Germany's withdrawal; weak action against **Italy's invasion of Abyssinia** and **Japan's invasion of Manchuria** → World War II.

## 8.3 World War II (September 1939 – August 1945)
### Causes
1. **Hitler's rise (Germany):** Weimar Republic failed; reparations → inflation, unemployment, 1929 depression. **Adolf Hitler** (Austrian; wrote **Mein Kampf** in prison) – **Chancellor 1933** (National Socialist Party); after Hindenburg died he took the presidency; **Reichstag fire** blamed on communists; abolished other parties; **Gestapo and SS**; propaganda under **Goebbels**; **Nazism** – Aryan superiority, anti-Jewish, party dictatorship. Withdrew from the League (1934). Annexed **Austria (1938)**, **Sudetenland and Czechoslovakia**; invaded **Poland** → war.
   - Concentration camps: **Auschwitz, Bergen-Belsen, Belzec, Sobibor**.
2. **Mussolini (Italy):** disappointed by peace settlement; **Fascist Party 1922**; **Black Shirts**; invaded **Abyssinia (Ethiopia) 1936**; joined Hitler.
3. **Japan:** needed raw materials and markets; **invaded Manchuria (1934 in the text; 1931 historically)**, left the League, took Chinese cities.
4. **Weakness of the League of Nations.**

### Sides
- **Allies:** Britain, France, USA, Soviet Russia (and others). **Axis (Berlin–Rome–Tokyo, 1937):** Germany, Italy, Japan.

### Course
- **1 September 1939** – Germany invaded **Poland** (defeated in 4 weeks); Britain and France declared war.
- Germany took Norway, Denmark, Holland, Belgium; **France (June 1940, Paris captured)**; Italy joined.
- **Churchill** united Britain and its Commonwealth; **Battle of Britain (14 Sept 1940)** – Germany lost ~56 aircraft; Hitler gave up the invasion.
- **Pearl Harbor** – Japan bombed the US fleet (text: 7 November 1941; the date is 7 December 1941) → **Franklin Roosevelt** declared war; Germany and Italy declared war on the USA → world war.
- Germany attacked **Russia** (like Napoleon, 125 years earlier); Russian winter, hunger, disease destroyed German forces.
- **Germany surrendered unconditionally in May 1945**; Hitler committed suicide.
- **Japan** conquered Singapore, Malaya, Burma, Hong Kong, Borneo, Java, Sumatra, Philippines by 1942; **bombed Colombo (5 April 1942) and Trincomalee (7 April 1942)**; British garrisoned Ceylon but no battle.
- **Atomic bombs: Hiroshima 6 August 1945; Nagasaki 9 August 1945** – more than 100 000 dead; **Emperor Hirohito** surrendered unconditionally.

- **Cold War ("weaponless war")** ~4 decades: spies, organisations, aid.
- **Non-Aligned Movement (1961)** – leaders **Nehru, Tito, Nasser**.

## 8.4 United Nations Organization (UN)
- **Atlantic Charter** (Churchill and Roosevelt, **August 1941**); **26 nations** signed the **Declaration of the United Nations (Washington, January 1942)**; **Moscow conference (Oct 1943)**; **Tehran (Dec 1943)** – Roosevelt, Churchill, Stalin; **San Francisco conference (June 1945)** – 50 countries + Poland = **51 founding members**; UN officially started **24 October 1945** (UN Day).
- **Aims:** international peace and security; friendship among nations; cooperation on human rights and independence.

![Fig 8.18 – United Nations headquarters, New York](Assets/Chapter_08_Figure_18.png)

![Fig 8.19 – Official logo of the United Nations](Assets/Chapter_08_Figure_19.png)

### Six main organs
| Organ | Details |
|---|---|
| **General Assembly** | All **193** members; one vote each; meets every **September**; discusses peace, approves budget, appoints Secretary-General (on Security Council's recommendation), elects ICJ judges, admits members |
| **Security Council** | **15 members** – 5 permanent (**USA, USSR/Russia, UK, France, China**) with **veto** + 10 elected for **2 years**; handles threats to peace; sanctions; military action |
| **Economic and Social Council (ECOSOC)** | **54 members**, 3-year terms; agencies: **ILO, FAO, UNESCO, IMF, WHO, UNICEF** |
| **Trusteeship Council** | Administered **11 trust territories**; all independent by **1994**; now inactive |
| **International Court of Justice** | **The Hague (Netherlands)**; legal advice and settling disputes |
| **Secretariat** | **New York**; **Secretary-General** appointed for **5 years**; about **9000 staff** |

### UN peace efforts
- **Atomic Energy Commission** (first step).
- **Suez conflict** – Nasser nationalised the Canal; Israel, Britain and France attacked; UN settled.
- **Gulf conflict** – Iraq invaded Kuwait (oil dispute); UN mediated.
- **Cuban Missile Crisis** – Soviet missiles in Cuba, US blockade and missiles in Turkey; settled by the UN Secretary-General.
- **Tools today:** economic sanctions; limiting nuclear weapons (commissions to North Korea and Iraq); **peacekeeping forces**.
